# Leviticus — Chapter-by-Chapter Summary (Christian Perspective)

**Chapter 1**
God calls Moses from the Tent of Meeting and lays down the first law of the sacrificial system: the burnt offering. The worshipper must choose an unblemished male animal from the herd, the flock, or from among the birds (turtledoves or young pigeons), and present it at the door of the Tent so that it may be accepted for him to make atonement. Laying his hand on the animal's head is a gesture of identification, transferring his guilt to the substitute. The priest sprinkles the blood around the altar and burns the whole sacrifice, an offering made by fire of a pleasant aroma to Yahweh. A Christian reads this as the first picture of the perfect sacrifice: Christ, who offered Himself entirely to the Father's acceptance. Though Israel could never offer a perfect animal for themselves, here the shadow points to the Lamb without blemish who would wholly give Himself. Even the poorest could offer birds, showing that God provides a way for every worshipper, just as the gospel is for the rich and poor alike.

**Chapter 2**
The meal offering of fine flour, oil, and frankincense may be baked in an oven, cooked on a griddle, or prepared in a pan. Part of it, the memorial portion, is burned on the altar as a pleasant aroma, while the remainder belongs to Aaron and his sons as the most holy part of the offerings made by fire. No leaven or honey may be burned on the altar, though both may be brought as first fruits, and every meal offering must be seasoned with the salt of the covenant. First fruits may be offered as parched, crushed grain with oil and frankincense. For Christians, this chapter foreshadows Christ as the true Bread from heaven, and the whole system prefigures believers offering their bodies as a living sacrifice, as Paul later commands. The ban on leaven points to sinlessness, since the Lord's Supper uses unleavened bread, and the salt recalls Christ as the "salt of the earth" and the covenant that is never dissolved.

**Chapter 3**
The peace offerings, called sacrifices of peace offerings, may come from the herd or the flock, male or female, and must be without defect. The worshipper lays his hand on the head of the animal and kills it at the door of the Tent, while the priests sprinkle the blood around the altar. The fat that covers the innards, the kidneys with their fat, the tail of the lamb, and the cover of the liver are burned on the altar as the portion belonging to the Lord, for all the fat is Yahweh's. A perpetual statute forbids eating fat or blood. Christians see in the peace offering a picture of reconciliation: the worshipper, the priest, and God share the same meal, foreshadowing fellowship with God through the blood of Christ. It also anticipates the Lord's Table, where believers commune with one another and with their Lord.

**Chapter 4**
Yahweh gives the law of the sin offering for unintentional sins: the anointed priest must offer a bull whose blood is sprinkled seven times before the veil and on the horns of the incense altar; the whole congregation's inadvertent sin is likewise atoned for with a bull brought by the elders; a ruler offers a male goat; and a common person offers a female goat or a lamb. In each case the priest makes atonement and the worshipper is forgiven, though the residue of the sacrifice is burned outside the camp. The graded alternatives show that God considers the worshipper's means, as He cares for the poor as much as the wealthy. From a Christian standpoint, this chapter underscores that even unintentional sin is sinful and requires atonement, and that Christ, the great High Priest, has made a perfect provision for sin. Hebrews later explains that the blood of these beasts only cleansed the flesh, while the blood of Christ cleanses the conscience from dead works.

**Chapter 5**
The first section lists offenses such as silence when called to testify, touching an unclean carcass, uncleanness hidden from oneself, and rash oaths, for which a female goat or lamb serves as a trespass offering. Those too poor for a lamb may offer two turtledoves or two young pigeons, one for sin and one for burnt offering, and the poorest may bring a tenth of an ephah of fine flour. A second section addresses trespass against the holy things of Yahweh: a ram is required, with restitution plus a fifth, and even unknowing trespass brings guilt. God thus gives a graduated scale of restitution and sacrifice, so that no burden of sin goes unaddressed. The Christian reader sees the heart of repentance here: confession, restitution, and the priest's work of atonement, all fulfilled in Christ who "himself bore our sins in his own body on the tree."

**Chapter 6**
God commands that the fire on the altar burn continually through the night, with the priest tending it and burning the fat of the peace offerings each morning, so that the flame never goes out. The memorial of the meal offering is burned on the altar, and the rest is eaten without yeast in the holy court by Aaron and his sons, who are holy by touch. Aaron himself and his anointed successors must offer a daily tenth of an ephah of flour, wholly burned as a perpetual statute. The law of the sin offering requires that it be killed where the burnt offering is killed, and be eaten only by the priest in a holy place; anything whose blood entered the Holy Place must be burned with fire. Christians view the never-extinguished fire as a picture of God's perpetual presence and the unceasing intercession of Christ, who ever lives to make intercession. The sharing of holy food foreshadows how communion unites believers with God's priestly provision.

**Chapter 7**
The chapter continues with the law of the trespass offering, which like the sin offering is most holy and eaten only by the priest in a holy place. The priest receives the skin of any burnt offering, and all meal offerings, whether baked, pan-made, or griddle-made, are divided among the sons of Aaron. Peace offerings given for thanksgiving are accompanied by unleavened and leavened bread, and must be eaten the same day; offerings of vow or free will may be eaten the next day but nothing may remain to the third. Eating meat that touches anything unclean, or meat while in a state of uncleanness, brings the eater's iniquity upon himself. Yahweh forbids eating fat and blood anywhere, and prescribes the waved breast and heaved thigh as the priests' portion of the peace offerings forever. The chapter closes by summarizing the ordinances given at Sinai. Christian readers note the repeated emphasis that the blood signifies life, and that the table fellowship is only for those who are clean in Christ.

**Chapter 8**
Moses consecrates Aaron and his sons in elaborate detail: they are washed, clothed in tunics, sashes, the robe, the ephod with its breastplate containing Urim and Thummim, and the holy crown. Moses anoints the tabernacle, the altar, the basin, and Aaron himself, sanctifying them all. The bull of the sin offering is killed and its blood purifies the altar; the burnt offering ram is wholly consumed; and from the ram of consecration, blood is applied to Aaron's and his sons' right ears, thumbs, and great toes, waved offerings are placed on the fat, and the anointing oil and blood are sprinkled over priest and garments. They must boil and eat the meat at the door of the Tent and not go outside for seven days. Christians see a foreshadowing of their own consecration: we are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, set apart in the blood and Spirit of Christ, marked for His service.

**Chapter 9**
On the eighth day Aaron begins ministering as priest, offering the sin offering for himself and the people, the burnt offerings, the meal offering, and the peace offerings, with his sons assisting in the details. After Aaron waves the breasts and lifts the right thigh as wave offerings, he raises his hands and blesses the people. Moses and Aaron then enter the Tent of Meeting and come out blessing Israel, when Yahweh's glory appears to all the people, and fire from before Him consumes the burnt offering and the fat on the altar. The congregation shouts and falls on their faces in awe. For Christians this day of fire is a vivid picture of God's acceptance of sacrifice, an anticipation of the cross, where the Lamb was accepted and the Father's fire of judgment was turned upon the offering for our sins.

**Chapter 10**
Nadab and Abihu offer "strange fire," which Yahweh had not commanded, and fire from before Him consumes them in the sanctuary. Moses declares that through this Yahweh shows Himself holy to those who come near Him, and Aaron keeps his silence before God's judgment. The bodies are carried outside the camp by Aaron's cousins, and the surviving priests are forbidden to mourn in the customary fashion of tearing clothes and disheveling their hair, lest the whole congregation die. Yahweh then gives a perpetual statute: priests must not drink wine or strong drink when entering the Tent, and must make a distinction between holy and common, clean and unclean. Moses later rebukes Eleazar and Ithamar for burning the goat of the sin offering rather than eating it in the sanctuary, though Aaron's plea, that such a solemn day of death should not be met with joyous eating, is accepted. Christians learn both the terror of God's holiness and the grace in which even Aaron's defense was honored, while Christ, the true High Priest, remained wholly undefiled before God.

**Chapter 11**
God tells Israel which animals may be eaten: land animals must both part the hoof and chew the cud, excluding the camel, hyrax, hare, and pig; fish must have fins and scales; birds of prey and unclean fowl are forbidden; and winged insects with jointed legs for hopping, such as locusts, crickets, and grasshoppers, are permitted. Rules of contact uncleanness follow, with washing of clothes and uncleanness until evening. The chapter ends with the great refrain: "I am Yahweh your God... You shall therefore be holy, for I am holy." Historically, these laws preserved Israel's health and marked them distinct among the nations; Christ as a man observed them during His earthly ministry. The New Testament, through Peter's vision and Paul's teaching, shows that the clean/unclean food distinction was a temporary ordinance shadowing spiritual truth, so that in Christ Jew and Gentile are both clean before God.

**Chapter 12**
A woman bearing a male child is unclean seven days, after which the child is circumcised on the eighth day, and she continues in the blood of purification for thirty-three days; for a female child the counts double to fourteen and sixty-six days. On the day of her purification she must bring a year-old lamb for a burnt offering and a young pigeon or turtledove for a sin offering; if she cannot afford a lamb, two birds suffice. The priest makes atonement and she is cleansed from the fountain of her blood. Christians recognize this chapter when Mary brings two pigeons to the temple (Luke 2:24), showing the humble poverty of the Holy Family. The blood of birth also pictures the stain of original sin that clings to every child of Adam, cleansed only through the blood of Christ.

**Chapter 13**
This long chapter gives the priest rules for diagnosing leprosy: swellings, scabs, bright spots, boils that heal into discoloration, burns that turn white, itch in the scalp or beard, and bald heads showing reddish-white lesions. Isolation and repeated examination for seven-day periods determine whether a mark is a scab or a plague; full-body whiteness paradoxically signals cleansing, but any raw flesh means uncleanness. The leper must wear torn clothes, let his hair hang loose, cover his upper lip, cry "Unclean! Unclean!", and dwell outside the camp. The law extends to mildew in garments of wool, linen, or leather, which must be isolated, washed, and burned if it spreads. For Christians, leprosy is the classic Old Testament symbol of sin's defilement and its social separation, and it is striking that Christ healed lepers on demand, even touching them in ways the Law forbade, declaring "I will; be clean."

**Chapter 14**
The cleansing ritual for a healed leper requires two live birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop: one bird is killed over running water in an earthen vessel, and the living bird, cedar, scarlet, and hyssop are dipped in the mingled blood and water and sprinkled seven times on the cleansed man, after which the living bird is set free. He must wash his clothes, shave all his hair, and bathe, remaining outside his tent for seven days while completing the shaving on the seventh day. On the eighth day he offers two male lambs, a year-old ewe lamb, meal offering, and oil, with blood and oil applied to his right ear, thumb, and great toe, echoing the priestly consecration. A poor man may substitute one lamb with flour, oil, and two turtledoves. The chapter concludes with laws for cleansed houses, using the same bird-and-hyssop ritual. Christian expositors richly connect these symbols, hyssop with the cross, cedar with the imperishable wood, running water with living water, and the scarlet with royalty, while Christ's pronouncement "Your sin is forgiven" on healing lepers reveals Him as the true cleanser of both disease and guilt.

**Chapter 15**
The chapter regulates bodily discharges: a man with a flux is unclean, as is anything he touches or lies upon, and earthenware he touches must be broken. After cleansing he counts seven days, washes in running water, and on the eighth day brings two turtledoves or young pigeons for sin and burnt offerings. A seminal emission renders a man unclean until evening, and sexual intercourse makes both partners unclean until evening. A woman with her period or abnormal bleeding is unclean, and all she touches becomes so; anyone who lies with her during her uncleanness is unclean seven days. When her discharge ends she counts seven days and offers birds on the eighth day. The purpose stated is that Israel should not die in their uncleanness by defiling the tabernacle that dwells among them. Jesus' encounter with the woman who had suffered twelve years with a flow of blood, whom He declared clean by touch of faith, shows how the Messiah transcends these shadows while confirming the need for purity before the holy God.

**Chapter 16**
God institutes the Day of Atonement immediately after the deaths of Nadab and Abihu, warning Aaron not to enter the Most Holy Place at just any time, for Yahweh appears in the cloud on the mercy seat. Aaron wears only linen holy garments and, having bathed, takes a bull for himself, two goats and a ram for the people, and casts lots: one goat is offered for sin, the other becomes the scapegoat. He burns incense so that the cloud covers the mercy seat, sprinkles the bulls' and goat's blood on and before the mercy seat seven times, and makes atonement for the Holy Place, the Tent, the altar, and the people. Over the live goat he confesses all the iniquities of Israel, sends it away into the wilderness to carry their sins to a solitary land, then washes, offers burnt offerings, and the two sin offerings are burned outside the camp. On the tenth day of the seventh month all Israelites must afflict their souls and do no work, a perpetual statute for atonement. Christians recognize in Hebrews chapters 9 and 10 the definitive interpretation: Christ entered the true Holy Place once for all, sprinkling our consciences with His own blood, better than goats' and bulls', and the scapegoat foreshadows Him bearing our sins far away.

**Chapter 17**
Yahweh commands that all slaughter of sacrificial animals be brought to the door of the Tent of Meeting for the priest, so that the blood is sprinkled on the altar and the fat burned for a pleasant aroma, ending sacrifices to goat idols. The theological heart of the chapter is that "the life of the flesh is in the blood," and the blood is given on the altar to make atonement for souls because it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life. Eating any blood, of bird or animal, incurs being cut off from the people, and even hunted game must have its blood poured out and covered with dust. Anyone who eats carrion must wash clothes and bathe, else he bears his iniquity. This is the clearest Old Testament statement that blood atones because life resides in it, a truth Christians read as pointing irresistibly to the Lord Jesus, who shed His own life-bearing blood for the redemption of many.

**Chapter 18**
God warns Israel, coming from Egypt and about to enter Canaan, not to copy the customs of either land but to walk in His statutes, since He who commands them is holy. He then enumerates forbidden intimacies: father, mother, father's wife, sister, granddaughters, father's and mother's sisters, father's brother, daughter-in-law, brother's wife, a woman and her daughter or granddaughters, a wife and her sister as rivals, and a menstruating woman. Also forbidden are giving children to Molech, lying with a man as with a woman, and bestiality. The land itself is said to vomit out its inhabitants for their abominations, so Israel must not follow them or face the same fate. The refrain throughout is "I am Yahweh your God." Christians read these ordinances as God's safeguard of marriage, the family, and human dignity, all confirmed and vindicated by Christ, who honored the Creator's design in Genesis and condemned the sexual sins of His day.

**Chapter 19**
The chapter opens with the summary of the whole book: "You shall be holy, for I Yahweh your God am holy." Israel must honor parents, keep the Sabbaths, avoid idols, and obey rules of harvest ethics, leaving corners and gleanings for the poor and the stranger. The Decalogue's spirit pervades it: do not steal, lie, deceive, swear falsely, oppress, or rob; pay hired workers the same day; show no partiality in judgment; do not slander, endanger a neighbor's life, hate in your heart, or take vengeance, but love your neighbor as yourself. Other regulations include not crossbreeding animals, not mixing seeds or fabrics, laws concerning a pledged slave-girl, the fruit of new trees forbidden three years, no enchantments or necromancy, no mourning cuts or tattoos, and protecting the stranger who becomes like a native-born. Christians treasure this chapter as the moral core of the Law, since Jesus Himself cited "love your neighbor as yourself" as the second greatest commandment and taught that the Law is fulfilled in love.

**Chapter 20**
Yahweh sets penalties for the serious violations listed before: death by stoning for giving offspring to Molech, for consulting mediums and wizards, for cursing a father or mother, for adultery, for incest with a father's wife, daughter-in-law, or sister, for male homosexual intercourse, for bestiality by either sex, and for taking a wife and her mother. The nation that hides its eyes to such sin will itself be cut off. The chapter reaffirms that Israelites must sanctify themselves because God sanctifies them, and that they must distinguish clean from unclean because Yahweh has set them apart from the peoples as His own. Christians view these penalties as expressing the gravity of sin under a theocratic covenant; in the New Covenant Christ bears the curse of the Law for transgressors so that they may be sanctified by His Spirit and made holy in Him.

**Chapter 21**
Priests are forbidden to defile themselves for the dead except for immediate family: mother, father, son, daughter, brother, and a virgin sister. They must not shave heads, shave corners of beards, or make cuttings in their flesh, because they offer the bread of their God and must be holy. They must not marry prostitutes, the profane, or divorced women, and the high priest, crowned with the anointing oil, has even stricter rules: no torn clothes, no entering to any corpse, and a wife must be a virgin of his own people. Finally, any descendant of Aaron with a physical defect, blindness, lameness, dwarfism, skin disease, and similar, may not approach the altar, though he may eat his priestly bread. Christians see in these requirements the portrait of Christ, the true High Priest, without blemish and undefiled, who entered once for all, and in the extension of priestly holiness to the whole church, "a holy priesthood" according to 1 Peter 2:9.

**Chapter 22**
Aaron and his sons are told to separate themselves from the holy things Israel consecrates, lest they profane God's holy name; any priest who touches something unclean must bathe and wait until evening before eating holy things. Lepers and those with discharges are excluded from holy food until clean. No stranger may eat the holy things, except a slave a priest has purchased or one born in the priest's household; a priest's daughter who marries outside may not eat the heave offerings unless she returns as a widow without children. One who eats a holy thing unwittingly must add a fifth to its value. Yahweh then commands that sacrificial animals be without defect; the defect may be offered freely but not to fulfill a vow; and a mother and her young may not be slain on the same day. Thanksgiving sacrifices must be eaten the same day, and the chapter ends with the purpose statement: "I will be made holy among the children of Israel." Christians apply this to worthy participation in the sacraments, approaching the table only after confession and reconciliation, and offering lives free of defect and hypocrisy.

**Chapter 23**
God declares the seven set feasts of Yahweh as holy convocations. The weekly Sabbath, Passover and Unleavened Bread in the first month, Firstfruits and the Feast of Weeks with its two leavened loaves and fifty-day count, Trumpets on the first of the seventh month, the Day of Atonement, and the Feast of Booths with its eight-day assembly and the palm, myrtle, willow, and citron. In Booths Israel dwells in temporary shelters to remember the wilderness sojourn. From a Christian perspective, the feasts unfold the story of redemption: Passover and the unleavened week proclaim Christ our Passover Lamb and His sinlessness; Firstfruits pictures His resurrection as the first of the harvest; Pentecost marks the Spirit and the birth of the church; Trumpets points to His return; Atonement to the cross's work of forgiveness; and Booths to the Incarnation, when God tabernacled among us, and to the coming kingdom.

**Chapter 24**
Israel must supply pure olive oil so that lamps burn continually outside the veil of the Testimony, and twelve loaves of showbread, six per row, with frankincense, must stand before Yahweh every Sabbath for Aaron and his sons to eat in a holy place, an everlasting covenant. The chapter then narrates the blasphemer, son of the Israelite Shelomith and an Egyptian father, who cursed the Name in the camp. Yahweh commands that those who heard him lay hands on his head and stone him, establishing the death penalty for blasphemy and for murder, along with the lex talionis, "fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth," applying one law to foreigner and native alike. The community carries out the sentence. Christians read the lamp as a shadow of the Spirit's illumination and the showbread of the daily provision of Christ, while the execution of the blasphemer anticipates the seriousness of the Holy Spirit's testimony about Christ, who alone forgives such sins.

**Chapter 25**
God institutes the Sabbath of the land every seventh year, when no sowing or reaping occurs and whatever grows feeds Israel, servants, strangers, and animals. Seven such cycles bring the fiftieth year, the Jubilee, proclaimed by trumpet on the Day of Atonement, when liberty is declared throughout the land and every one returns to his family and property. Land values are calculated according to the years remaining to the Jubilee, since the land belongs to God and may not be sold in perpetuity. Redemption of fields, walled-city houses (redeemable within a year, else permanent), and village houses (released at the Jubilee) is detailed, and Levites may redeem their cities at any time. Interest and profit must not be taken from a brother, and an impoverished Israelite who sells himself serves as a hired man until the Jubilee, when he and his children go free. Foreign slaves may be owned permanently. Christians hear in Jesus' own program (Luke 4:18-19) the citation of Isaiah's Jubilee text: "to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord," for His ministry releases what sin had lost, debts are cancelled, and captives go free in Christ.

**Chapter 26**
The covenant's blessings are lavish for obedience: seasonal rain, abundant harvest, peace, freedom from evil animals and sword, multiplication, and God's dwelling among His people, who will eat the new while casting out the old. Disobedience brings escalating curses seven times over: terror, consumption, vain labor, iron skies, wild animals devouring children, siege and famine, even the abomination of eating one's own children, desolated cities, and exile until the desolate land enjoys its missed Sabbaths. In the nations Israel's remnant will be faint-hearted, fleeing without pursuers. Yet the chapter closes with extraordinary mercy: if the remnant confesses their sins and their fathers', if their uncircumcised hearts are humbled, God will remember the covenants with Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham, and will neither reject nor utterly destroy them, remembering the exodus. Christians see in the curses a sober picture of judgment, and in the closing mercy the gospel: God's promises are kept in Christ, who remembers His covenant with the fathers and does not let His people perish, but seals them with the new covenant, "forgiving their iniquities and remembering their sins no more."

**Chapter 27**
The final chapter regulates vows and dedications. Valuations of persons given to Yahweh are fixed by age and sex: fifty shekels for a man aged twenty to sixty, thirty for a woman, decreasing with age down to fifteen and ten for those over sixty, with the priest assessing poorer individuals. Animals, houses, and fields dedicated become holy; they may not be swapped for better or worse, but may be redeemed by adding a fifth to the valuation. Fields dedicated between Jubilees are valued by the years remaining, and unredeemed devoted fields pass to the priests. Firstborn animals already belong to the Lord, redeemable only with the fifth added. Things wholly devoted, a person, animal, or field, are most holy and may never be sold or ransomed, and one devoted from among men must surely be put to death. Finally, the tithe of the land and of every tenth animal passing under the rod belongs to Yahweh as holy, redeemable only with the fifth added. Christians understand these laws as teaching that everything belongs to God, that our tithes and vows are acts of devotion, and that Christ is the ultimate one dedicated, redeemed, and given for our ransom, so that we may freely return to Him all we are.
