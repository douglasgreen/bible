Write a Christian commentary on this chapter of Matthew.

# New Testament, World English Bible

## Matthew

### Chapter 1

#### Introduction: A Gospel That Begins with a Legal Document

Where other ancient biographies open with a dramatic scene, Matthew opens with a family register. "The book of the genealogy" (v. 1) is no casual introduction; it is the literary equivalent of unrolling a deed in a courtroom. Before telling us what Jesus *did*, Matthew establishes who Jesus *is*—and he does so by anchoring him to the two great pillars of Israel's hope: **Abraham**, through whom God promised to bless all the families of the earth (Gen. 12:1–3), and **David**, to whom God promised an everlasting throne (2 Sam. 7:12–16). To call Jesus "the son of David, the son of Abraham" is to claim that in him the covenant promises made centuries earlier have finally come home.

#### The Genealogy (vv. 1–17)

**Three sets of fourteen.** Matthew arranges the history of Israel into three periods of fourteen generations each: from Abraham to David, from David to the exile, and from the exile to Christ (v. 17). In Hebrew letter-numbers, the name David (דוד) totals fourteen, so the structure quietly points to David's line as the key to the whole. The number seven signifies completeness; fourteen, its double, suggests a completed story reaching its goal. Matthew counts inclusively (Abraham and David are counted in two groups each), which tells us he is shaping history *theologically* rather than doing strict arithmetic: God's dealings with Israel fall into three acts—promise, kingdom, exile—and now the final act is closing with the arrival of the Messiah.

**The five women.** What would have scandalized a first-century reader is that four women appear in this royal line: Tamar (v. 3), Rahab (v. 5), Ruth (v. 5), and Bathsheba, whom Matthew pointedly calls only "her who had been Uriah's wife" (v. 6). Three of them were Gentiles—Canaanite, Canaanite, and Moabitess—and each entered the line through circumstances that were irregular, even scandalous: Tamar's disguised approach to Judah, Rahab's profession as a prostitute, Ruth's presence on the threshing floor, Bathsheba's adultery. Matthew names no woman by her sin except in the case of David, and he refuses even to name Bathsheba, as if to say that grace has covered this page too. The message is unmistakable: **the Messiah's family tree is a tree of mercy, not merit.** It foreshadows everything that follows in this Gospel—the Gentile wise men (ch. 2), the Canaanite woman (ch. 8), the call to the Gentiles (chs. 10, 28). God's saving line runs through broken people, and it will run through broken nations.

**The break at the exile.** Note how the genealogy handles the catastrophe of 586 B.C. "Josiah became the father of Jechoniah and his brothers *at the time of the exile*" (v. 11). The line is not cut off, but it is wounded. Jeremiah had pronounced a curse on Jehoiachin's descendants (Jer. 22:30), yet Matthew includes him and continues the chain—God's promise outlasts even his own pronouncement of judgment. After the exile, something striking happens: the names are no longer kings. Shealtiel and Zerubbabel were governors, not monarchs. The throne of David stands empty for generations, waiting. The genealogy ends not with a king but with Joseph, "the husband of Mary, from whom was born Jesus" (v. 16). Legally, through Joseph's acknowledgment and adoption, Jesus inherits David's throne; biologically, he descends through Mary, who is herself of David's line (cf. Luke 3:23–38). Matthew thus presents the Messiah as both the rightful heir and the true seed of David.

#### The Birth Narrative (vv. 18–25)

**"Before they came together."** The genealogy's final link is now unpacked in story form. Mary was found to be with child "by the Holy Spirit" (v. 18)—a conception without human father, the work of God himself entering history. This is not a myth about a god's descent; it is the announcement that the Creator has become a son of David.

**Joseph the righteous.** Joseph's response is one of the quietest heroisms in Scripture. Faced with what appeared to be his fiancée's adultery—a capital offense under the law (Deut. 22:22)—he did not rush to the crowd or the court. "Being a righteous man, and not willing to make her a public example, he intended to put her away secretly" (v. 19). His righteousness is not legalism but mercy; his first instinct is to protect Mary's honor even while obeying the law. And when God speaks to him in a dream, Joseph obeys without recorded question. He "arose from his sleep, and did as the angel of the Lord commanded him" (v. 24). Matthew will give us no speeches from Joseph; his faithfulness is shown entirely in what he *does*. In a Gospel that ends with a command to make disciples, it is fitting that it opens with a man who simply obeyed.

**The name Jesus.** The angel's instruction carries the soteriology of the whole Gospel: "You shall name him Jesus, for it is he who shall save his people from their sins" (v. 21). *Yeshua* means "the LORD saves." Notice what the salvation is *from*: not merely from Rome, from famine, or from death, but from **sin**. Matthew's Messiah is first and foremost a savior of souls, and this sets the stage for everything that follows—the cross as atonement (ch. 27), the confession of Peter ("You are the Christ, the Son of the living God," 16:16), and the final commission to teach obedience to all that Jesus commanded (28:20).

**"God with us."** Matthew then quotes Isaiah 7:14: "Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and shall give birth to a son. They shall call his name Immanuel," which means "God with us" (vv. 22–23). In its original context, this sign was given to King Ahaz during the crisis of the Syro-Ephraimite war—a promise that God would be present with his people in judgment and deliverance. Matthew reads it messianically: the sign has now reached its fullness. The name Immanuel is a banner over the entire Gospel, and Matthew frames his book with it: the Gospel opens with "God with us" (1:23) and closes with "I am with you always, even to the end of the age" (28:20). Between those two statements stands the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

**"Until she had given birth."** The chapter closes with a careful formula: Joseph "didn't know her sexually until she had given birth to her firstborn son" (v. 25). This confirms both the virginity of the conception and the full reality of Jesus' humanity—he was born, he was circumcised as a firstborn under the law (Exod. 13:2), he was named, he entered the covenant community as any Israelite child. The God who is "with us" is not a distant presence but an infant in a Bethlehem home.

#### Summary and Application

Matthew 1 teaches us four things worth carrying into our own lives:

1. **God keeps his promises across generations.** From Abraham to Jesus, the chain of promise runs through judges, kings, exiles, and sinners—and it is never broken. If God has been faithful for three thousand years, he will be faithful for your lifetime.
2. **Grace, not pedigree, marks God's family.** The Messiah's ancestry includes prostitutes, Gentiles, and adulterers. No one is beyond the reach of the grace that runs through this line.
3. **Obedience is often quiet.** Joseph never utters a word in the Gospels, yet his silent "yes" carried the Son of God into the world. Faithfulness to what God has already commanded is its own kind of heroism.
4. **The name Jesus means salvation from sin.** The whole story of this Gospel—and of our own—turns on that one claim: he saves *from sin*. To call him by his name is to receive what the angel promised.

The genealogy ends with a man named Joseph; the chapter ends with a child named Jesus. Between those two names stands the entire hope of Scripture: that God, who made covenants with Abraham and David, has come himself—born of a virgin, raised in Nazareth, "God with us"—to save his people from their sins.

### Chapter 2

#### Introduction

If chapter 1 presents the birth of Christ as the fulfillment of God's covenant with David, chapter 2 presents it as the beginning of a conflict between two kingdoms: the throne of Herod and the throne of the Messiah. The chapter is built around four movements — the coming of the Magi, the flight into Egypt, the slaughter of the innocents, and the settlement at Nazareth — and it carries four "fulfillment" formulas (vv. 4–6, 15, 17, 23), Matthew's signature device for showing that Jesus is the center of Scripture's whole story.

Three themes run throughout: **Christ's kingship** (which the world's rulers fear and the nations worship), **the reversal of expectation** (Gentile seekers find what Israel's leaders fail to seek), and **Jesus as the true Israel**, who recapitulates his people's history — exile in Egypt, return from it, and a voice of lament over slain children.

---

#### I. The Magi Come to Jerusalem (vv. 1–6)

**"In the days of King Herod"** fixes the scene under Herod the Great, Rome's client king, who died in 4 BC — which is why most scholars place the birth a few years before AD 1. Bethlehem, David's own town, is the stage; but the first visitors are not shepherds (as in Luke) but *magi* from the east — almost certainly Persian or Babylonian astrologers, men of the ancient science that read heaven for signs.

Their question is remarkable: "Where is he who is born King of the Jews?" They do not ask whether a king has been born; they assume it. Their knowledge likely came from the Jewish diaspora in Mesopotamia — the land of Daniel — where Jewish and Gentile learning mingled. Many have connected their expectation to Balaam's oracle, "There shall come a Star out of Jacob" (Num. 24:17). Whatever the precise source, the point is that God had so scattered his Word among the nations that even pagan astronomers were led by it toward the Messiah.

Note what Matthew does *not* say: he never gives their number or their names. The tradition of three — Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar — grew up later from the three gifts and became part of the Church's Epiphany devotion (January 6). But the text leaves them as representatives of the nations coming to worship.

**Verse 3** is a masterstroke of contrast. When Herod hears of a new Jewish king, "he was troubled, and all Jerusalem with him." His anxiety is political, not spiritual: any claimant to David's throne threatened his own (he was an Idumean usurper, not a Jew by blood). The whole city trembles — yet the child in Bethlehem sleeps on.

In **vv. 4–6**, Herod summons "all the chief priests and scribes" and asks where the Christ is to be born. Their answer is precise: Bethlehem, citing Micah 5:2 — "Out of you shall come a ruler who will shepherd my people Israel." The irony is deliberate and severe. These men can locate the Messiah in Scripture but do not go to worship him. They know the text; they miss the King. It is a standing warning to every age: one may handle prophecy accurately and yet never kneel.

---

#### II. Herod's Deception and the Star's Guidance (vv. 7–12)

Herod "secretly" summons the Magi — secrecy being the telltale sign of his heart. He learns the exact date of the star's appearance, not to honor it but to set the age-limit for a massacre. His invitation — "that I also may come and worship him" — is one of Scripture's great examples of feigned piety: the language of devotion in the service of murder.

The Magi, however, are led by grace. The star "went before them until it came and stood over where the young child was." Matthew records this plainly as divine guidance; the ancient Church read it as a miracle of providence, and rightly so — no natural phenomenon "stands" over a house. When they see it again, "they rejoiced with exceedingly great joy," an emphatic doubling that marks the joy of those who have long sought and at last found.

Two details deserve attention:

- **They enter "a house."** This is not Luke's stable. The family has evidently returned to their home in Bethlehem (Joseph's ancestral town, cf. Ruth 1:22) some time after the birth — consistent with the two-year limit Herod later sets.
- **The gifts: gold, frankincense, and myrrh.** The Church has long read these as a summary of Christ's whole mission. Gold speaks of his **kingship**; frankincense, used in the temple, speaks of his **divinity and priestly intercession**; myrrh, an embalming spice, speaks of his **death** (cf. John 19:39). In one scene the Magi confess — by act rather than creed — that this child is King, God, and one who will die. Their prostration before a human infant is, in Matthew's Gospel, an act of worship owed to God alone.

Then comes the first of the chapter's dreams: they are "warned in a dream not to return to Herod" and depart "another way." Grace protects them from the king's trap. The nations' seekers go home transformed; Israel's rulers remain where they were.

---

#### III. The Flight into Egypt (vv. 13–15)

Now the angel turns to Joseph: "Arise, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt." Matthew's pattern is consistent — God governs through dreams and angels in a world of human danger (cf. 1:20, 28). Joseph obeys "by night," without delay, as one who trusts even when he does not yet understand.

The family becomes refugees. The Son of the King flees as a fugitive; the Mother of God crosses a border with her infant. Here Matthew makes his deepest theological move in the chapter. He cites **Hosea 11:1**: "Out of Egypt I called my son." In Hosea, that word refers to Israel's Exodus — God calling his son, the nation, out of bondage. Matthew applies it to Jesus *typologically*: in Christ, Israel's story is recapitulated and fulfilled. He goes down into Egypt and comes up again; he is the true Son who does what the first son could not permanently do. The Messiah is at once the Son of David and the true Israel — the one in whom God's people finds its identity and its deliverance.

---

#### IV. The Slaughter of the Innocents (vv. 16–18)

Herod, "when he saw that he was mocked by the wise men," unleashes his rage on the only victims within his reach: "all the male children in Bethlehem and in all the surrounding countryside, from two years old and under."

A word of historical honesty is in order. Josephus, who records Herod's many cruelties (he murdered his own wife and sons), does not mention this slaughter — but Bethlehem was a small town, and the number of children within the age-limit was likely small, perhaps only a score or so. The silence of other sources is not surprising; the horror for those families was total either way.

Matthew cites **Jeremiah 31:15**: "A voice was heard in Ramah, lamentation and great mourning — Rachel weeping for her children." Ramah, near Bethlehem, was associated with Rachel's tomb (Gen. 35:19) and with the weeping of mothers whose sons were carried into exile after Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC. Matthew applies that ancient grief to this new one: the mother's lament is not over; it has returned in the shadow of the Messiah's birth.

Two things must be held together here. First, **evil is real and costly**. Innocent children die because of one man's pride — a truth the Church has never softened, honoring these children as her first martyrs (their feast is kept December 28). Second, **God does not abandon the tragedy to despair**. Jeremiah's lament sits inside a promise: "There is hope for your future," says the Lord (Jer. 31:17). In Christian hope, the blood of the innocents cries out and is answered — these children share in the resurrection of the one whose birth occasioned their death. The darkest verse in the infancy narrative points forward to Easter.

---

#### V. The Return to Nazareth (vv. 19–23)

Herod dies, and the angel summons the family home: "those who sought the child's life are dead." But Joseph learns that **Archelaus**, Herod's son, now rules Judea — a ruler of such brutality that Rome would depose him within a decade. "He was afraid to go there," and, warned in a dream, settles instead in Galilee, "in a city called Nazareth."

The final fulfillment formula is the most debated: "that he should be called a Nazarene." No exact Old Testament prophecy matches the phrase. Two readings have stood the test of time. One sees a wordplay on *netzer*, "branch" or "shoot" — Isaiah's messianic "Branch from the stump of Jesse" (Isa. 11:1) and the "tender shoot" of the Suffering Servant (Isa. 53:2). The other, simpler reading takes the phrase at face value: the prophets had spoken of a Messiah who would be identified with Nazareth — that is, with obscurity, contempt, and the scandal of Galilee ("Can anything good come from Nazareth?" John 1:46). Either way, Matthew's point is theological: **God's plan is not frustrated by human politics.** The child meant to reign does not take a throne in Jerusalem but grows up in a village no one counts.

---

#### Summary and Application

Matthew 2 compresses the whole Gospel into a single chapter. In it we see:

1. **The King born, and two responses to him.** The Magi worship; Herod plots murder. Every generation must choose between these postures. What would we lay at his feet — gold, incense, myrrh?
2. **The danger of knowing without worshipping.** The chief priests and scribes quoted Micah correctly and went home empty-handed. Scripture is not a subject to be mastered but a Person to be met.
3. **Providence in the dark.** God guides by star, angel, and dream — through exile, fear, and border crossings. Joseph's quiet obedience "by night" is the model of faith that moves before it understands.
4. **The cost of evil, and the hope that outlasts it.** The massacre reminds us that sin has victims, usually the smallest; but Rachel's weeping is heard by the God who raises the dead.
5. **Humility as the path of kingship.** The Messiah's first address is a house in Bethlehem, his childhood home a despised village in Galilee. He comes not to sit above the world but among its low places — which is how he will reign from a cross.

The chapter ends where the Gospel begins: in Nazareth, with a child growing up under God's watchful care, "that it might be fulfilled." The star has stood over the house; the question it poses to every reader is the Magi's question, answered and still open — *Where is he who is born King of the Jews?* He is there. Come and worship.

### Chapter 3

#### Introduction

Matthew 3 is the hinge of the Gospel. Chapters 1–2 have presented Jesus as the Son of David and the Son of Joseph, born in Bethlehem and preserved for his mission. Chapter 3 introduces him as the **Son of God**. The chapter moves in three great movements: the herald (John), the message (repentance before judgment), and the King (the baptized Messiah). It is a chapter about preparation — the way being made ready, and then the Lord himself arriving to walk it.

#### I. The Herald in the Wilderness (vv. 1–6)

**"In those days"** (v. 1) is a phrase Matthew borrows from the Old Testament (cf. Gen. 37:2; 1 Kings 12:19). It signals that a new era of salvation history has dawned, as it did when Joseph went down to Egypt or when Rehoboam took the throne. The long silence of the infancy narrative ends; the story of redemption moves forward.

John's location is no accident. Israel's decisive encounters with God have always occurred in the wilderness — Moses at the burning bush, Elijah on Horeb. By preaching in the wilderness of Judea, John evokes the **new exodus**: just as Israel was formed in the desert before entering the land, so a new people is being gathered in the desert before entering the kingdom.

His cry, **"Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand,"** compresses the whole gospel into two words. *Repent* (Greek *metanoeite*) means more than feeling sorry; it is a change of mind and direction — turning from rebellion toward God. And the reason given is not merely moral but eschatological: God's reign is breaking in. The call to repentance makes sense only because the King is near. Matthew's distinctive phrase "Kingdom of Heaven" (rather than "Kingdom of God") reflects a reverent Hebraic way of speaking of God's name, and it will become one of his great themes.

Matthew then applies Isaiah 40:3 to John — his first fulfillment formula ("For this is he who was spoken of by the prophet"). In its context, Isaiah speaks of preparing the way for **Yahweh himself** to return from exile. Matthew's point is staggering: the one coming to make straight the paths in the wilderness is none other than the Lord. John is not merely a moral reformer; he is the eschatological forerunner promised in Malachi 3:1, and (as Jesus will later confirm) the Elijah who was to come (Matt. 17:10–13).

John's appearance — **camel's hair and a leather belt** — deliberately recalls Elijah (2 Kings 1:8). His food, **locusts and wild honey**, is simple wilderness sustenance, evoking manna in the desert (Exod. 16) and signaling that this prophet lives by God's provision, outside the temple economy. The crowds' response is remarkable: people from Jerusalem, all Judea, and the whole region of the Jordan stream out to him. The religious center of the nation is drawn to a hermit in the desert. They are **baptized in the Jordan, confessing their sins** — a public, one-time act of acknowledgment that they have been living apart from God and now turn back to him.

#### II. The Rebuke of the Religious Elite (vv. 7–12)

When John sees many Pharisees and Sadducees coming, his response is not polite but cutting: **"You offspring of vipers."** In Jewish thought the serpent was the emblem of deception and evil (Gen. 3; Isa. 11:8). John sees through religious respectability. Their question — implicitly, "What should we do?" — he answers with a counter-question: **"Who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?"** Genuine repentance is not social conformity or curiosity about a new movement; it is flight from God's coming judgment, born of fear of the Lord.

His demand — **"produce fruit worthy of repentance"** — is the test of authenticity. A changed mind must produce a changed life. (Luke 3:10–14 gives us the specifics: honest dealing for tax collectors, contentment and fairness for soldiers.) Repentance without fruit is counterfeit.

The Pharisees' appeal to pedigree — **"We have Abraham for our father"** — receives one of the sharpest answers in the Gospels. Descent from Abraham guarantees nothing. **God is able to raise up children to Abraham from these stones.** True children of Abraham are defined not by blood but by faith (Gal. 3:7, 9; Rom. 9:6–8). The image that follows is grim and final: **the ax lies at the root of the trees**; every tree that does not bear good fruit will be cut down and cast into the fire. Judgment is imminent, and it will be decisive. Yet note John's pastoral heart: his severity is in service of mercy. He strikes hard precisely so that they may turn before the axe falls.

#### III. The Mightier One (vv. 11–12)

John now points beyond himself. **"I indeed baptize you in water for repentance"** — his ministry is preparatory, a sign pointing past itself to what it cannot accomplish. Water cleanses the outside; it cannot change the heart. That is the work of the one coming: **"He will baptize you in the Holy Spirit."** This is the promise of internal transformation — a new heart and a new spirit (Ezek. 36:25–27) — fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 1:5; 2:4) and applied to every believer since.

John's humility is striking: the one coming is **"mightier than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry."** Carrying a master's sandals was the lowest slave's task; John places himself below even that service to Jesus.

The final image is agricultural and judicial. The Messiah holds a **winnowing fork**: he will toss the harvested grain into the air so the wind blows away the chaff, thoroughly cleansing his threshing floor. The wheat — the redeemed — is gathered into the barn; the chaff — the rejected — is burned with **unquenchable fire** (cf. Isa. 66:24). The coming of the kingdom brings both harvest and judgment. This is why the gospel is good news that carries a warning: the same event that saves the wheat consumes the chaff.

#### IV. The Baptism of Jesus (vv. 13–17)

**"Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan."** Matthew's narrative turns on this moment. Why does the sinless Son submit to a baptism of repentance? John's protest — **"I need to be baptized by you, and you come to me?"** — voices the right question. His answer is the key to the whole chapter:

> "Allow it now, for this is the fitting way for us to fulfill all righteousness."

*Righteousness* here means God's ordained order, his saving plan as a whole. Jesus submits to it in at least three ways:

1. **Identification with his people.** He stands in the line of penitents, taking his place among sinners — not because he has sin (Heb. 4:15), but because he will bear it. This is the first echo of the cross, where he becomes what we are not, so that we may become what he is (2 Cor. 5:21).
2. **Typological fulfillment.** As Israel passed through the Jordan under Joshua into the Promised Land (Josh. 3–4), so the true Israel — Jesus, the faithful representative of his people — passes through the water into the new covenant age.
3. **Obedient inauguration.** His public ministry begins not with a claim but with submission to the Father's appointed order.

What follows is one of the great theophanies of Scripture. **Jesus went up directly from the water, and behold, the heavens were opened.** The language recalls Genesis 7:11, when the fountains of the great deep burst open in judgment; here the barrier between heaven and earth — closed since the fall — is torn open, and heaven invades earth.

**The Spirit of God descended as a dove and came on him.** This is an anointing without measure (John 3:34). The image echoes creation (Gen. 1:2), when the Spirit hovered over the waters — Jesus is the new Adam, in whom creation begins again — and it recalls the succession of prophets, for Elijah's mantle passed to Elisha as the Spirit rested upon him (2 Kings 2). Jesus is the prophet greater than Moses, anointed for a mission greater than any that has gone before.

Then **a voice out of the heavens** declares: **"This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased."** The words fuse two Old Testament texts: Psalm 2:7, where God crowns his anointed King ("You are my Son; today I have become your Father"), and Isaiah 42:1, where the Lord introduces his Servant, in whom he is well pleased. Matthew deliberately yokes kingship and servanthood together: the Messiah who comes to reign is the one who comes to serve — and, as chapter 20 will make explicit, to give his life as a ransom for many.

The scene is explicitly threefold: the Son in the water, the Spirit descending, the Father speaking. It is a rare, full manifestation of the Trinity (cf. Matt. 28:19), and it publicly answers Israel's ancient question — *Where is the Son of David?* He is here, approved by the Father, empowered by the Spirit.

Notice, too, what happens immediately afterward: in Matthew 4:1, **Jesus is led up into the wilderness by the Spirit**. Israel failed in her wilderness testing; the true Israel will succeed. The way has been made ready — and now the Lord walks it.

#### Conclusion and Application

Matthew 3 leaves us with several enduring convictions:

- **Repentance is real only when it bears fruit.** A profession without transformed conduct is a tree that will not survive the ax.
- **No pedigree, no religious heritage, guarantees salvation.** Only faith in the coming King counts.
- **The gospel is both invitation and warning.** The same Messiah who gathers wheat into the barn burns chaff with unquenchable fire; we are to receive his grace while there is time.
- **Christ's identification with sinners is the pattern of all grace.** He entered our water so that we might be brought into his life — baptized, as John promised, in the Holy Spirit.
- **"With whom I am well pleased"** is not only the Father's verdict on the Son; it is the goal of every Christian life (2 Cor. 5:9). To live by faith in Christ is to walk under that same approving voice.

The chapter ends where the next begins: heaven opened, the Spirit given, the Son approved — and the King setting his face toward the wilderness, to be tested, and to triumph.

### Chapter 4

#### Introduction: The Hinge of the Gospel

Matthew 4 stands at the threshold of Jesus's public ministry. Having presented his Messiah by genealogy (ch. 1), by birth and flight to Egypt (ch. 2), and by baptism and heavenly attestation (3:13–17), Matthew now brings us to the wilderness, where the Son of God is tested as Israel once was tested. The chapter's movement is deliberate: from victory over the tempter (vv. 1–11), to withdrawal into the land of light (vv. 12–16), to the proclamation of the Kingdom (v. 17), to the calling of the first disciples (vv. 18–22), and finally to a sweeping summary of Jesus's teaching, preaching, and healing ministry (vv. 23–25). What begins with one man alone in the desert ends with multitudes from the whole known world following him.

---

#### I. The Temptation in the Wilderness (vv. 1–11)

##### "Led up by the Spirit" (v. 1)

Matthew's opening words are rich with echo. Israel was led by God into the wilderness for forty years (Deut. 8:2); now Jesus, the true Son of God, is *led*—not driven, not abandoned—by the Holy Spirit into the same arena of testing. The verb suggests a gentle but purposeful guidance, as of a shepherd with his flock. There is also a quiet contrast with Genesis: in the garden, Adam was left to his own resources and failed; here the Son is accompanied by the Spirit from the first moment (cf. Gen. 1:2; John 1:32). The wilderness is not where God abandons his people but where he forms them—and it is precisely there that the enemy comes to make his accusation.

##### Forty Days and Hunger (v. 2)

Jesus fasts for forty days and nights, recalling both Israel's forty years and Elijah's forty-day journey to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8). His hunger afterward is no weakness of character but the honest condition of a true man. The tempter will exploit exactly this: *If you are the Son of God*—the very title the Father spoke from heaven at the Jordan, now wielded as a challenge. Each temptation begins with that conditional phrase, as though sonship were a credential to be cashed in for advantage rather than a relationship to be honored in obedience.

##### First Temptation: Bread (vv. 3–4)

The first appeal is to physical need and to the misuse of divine power. Turn the stones to bread—use your sonship for self-advantage, and prove it by a miracle. Matthew's readers will recognize the scene from Israel's history: in the wilderness God fed his people with manna, and they grumbled, testing him (Exod. 16). Jesus answers with Deuteronomy 8:3: *"Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds out of God's mouth."*

The quotation is a masterstroke of self-application. Israel learned in the wilderness that life comes from dependence on God's spoken word; Jesus *is* that man who lives by every word of God. He refuses to eat apart from the Father's will—not because bread is unimportant (he will later feed five thousand, and his own body will be given as food for the world), but because the Son does not seize his inheritance by force. The kingdom will come through obedience, not through a display of power detached from the Father's timing.

##### Second Temptation: The Pinnacle (vv. 5–7)

The devil now takes him to Jerusalem, the holy city, and sets him on the pinnacle of the temple—the highest point of the house of God. Here the tempter quotes Scripture itself, Psalm 91:11–12, correctly but fatally misapplied. The psalm promises God's protection *within* a life of trust; the devil would twist it into a warrant for presumption: throw yourself down and force God to rescue you, producing a spectacular proof of sonship.

Jesus answers with Deuteronomy 6:16: *"You shall not test the Lord your God."* The reference is to Massah (Exod. 17:1–7), where Israel demanded water and said, "Is the Lord among us or not?" Testing God is treating him as a means to an end, demanding that he confirm what we already want to believe. True faith receives protection from God; it does not extort it. The Son of God will indeed be lifted up—but on the cross, in the Father's hands and on the Father's terms, not by his own leap from the temple.

##### Third Temptation: The Kingdoms (vv. 8–10)

The final temptation is the gravest, for it concerns the very heart of the Messiah's mission. On an exceedingly high mountain the devil shows Jesus *all the kingdoms of the world and their glory* and offers to give them—on one condition: fall down and worship me. The offer is diabolically plausible. A kingdom without a cross, glory without suffering, dominion without death—what Messiah could refuse?

Yet the offer betrays its source. The devil has no title to what he offers; he is a usurper (cf. John 14:30; 2 Cor. 4:4). His "glory" is the world's present, fallen splendor under his stewardship—and it is temporary. Jesus's answer is twofold. First, a personal rejection: *"Get behind me, Satan!"* (literally, "Behind me, Satan"—get away from me; go back where you belong). The Son will not negotiate with the enemy of God. Then the scriptural warrant, Deuteronomy 6:13: *"You shall worship the Lord your God, and you shall serve him only."* Worship and service are indivisible; there is no middle path of using the devil's power while keeping one's heart for God. Israel fell to Baal Peor in the wilderness (Deut. 4:3); the true Son stands where they fell.

##### The Pattern of Victory (vv. 1–11)

Three temptations, three answers, all beginning with *"It is written."* Matthew's readers see the design: each temptation corresponds to one of Israel's wilderness failures—hunger and grumbling (Exod. 16), testing at Massah (Exod. 17), and idolatry (Deut. 4:3; 6:13). Where Israel failed, Jesus obeys. He is the true Son of God who does what the son of Israel could not: he lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God. The chapter's opening thus establishes the whole gospel in miniature—this is a Messiah who wins his throne through faithful obedience to Scripture and to the Father, not through power seized or compromise made.

##### "Angels Came and Served Him" (v. 11)

The devil departs—not defeated in himself, for he will return at Gethsemane and again at the cross—but repulsed from this man. And behold, angels come and serve him. The contrast with Eden is unmistakable: after Adam's fall he was left alone to face his shame; after Jesus's victory he is ministered to by heaven itself. There is also a quiet echo of Psalm 91, which the devil had misused: God's angelic protection is real, but it comes in its own way and time, to those who trust rather than test.

---

#### II. Withdrawal to Capernaum (vv. 12–16)

##### "When Jesus Heard That John Was Delivered Up" (v. 12)

The scene shifts abruptly. John the Baptist has been handed over to Herod—imprisoned, and soon to be executed (cf. 14:1–12). Jesus *withdraws* into Galilee. This is not flight from danger but a movement of divine timing: the hour for open confrontation with Judean authorities has not yet come, and the ministry must now take root in the north. Matthew's readers will later see that withdrawal can be obedience—Jesus himself will say, "I went away to him who sent me" (John 16:28).

##### The Fulfillment of Isaiah (vv. 13–16)

Leaving Nazareth, Jesus settles in Capernaum by the sea, in the territory of Zebulun and Naphtali—and Matthew declares that this is the fulfillment of Isaiah's prophecy (Isa. 9:1–2; LXX 8:17–18). The quotation is one of the most tender passages in the Old Testament, and its setting here is full of meaning.

Isaiah wrote these words after the fall of the northern kingdom, when Galilee had been devastated by Assyria (2 Kings 15:29; 17:6). The "land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali" were the first regions crushed in the Assyrian invasion. Yet the prophet promised that this very region would not remain in darkness: *"The people who sat in darkness saw a great light; to those who sit in the region and shadow of death, light has dawned."* The promise was messianic—a Davidic king whose peaceable reign would bring light even to the humbled north (Isa. 9:5–6).

Matthew adds one word that changes everything: *Galilee of the Gentiles.* In Isaiah's day the phrase described a borderland mixed with pagan peoples; in Matthew's day it points forward to the gospel crossing the Jordan and the sea to reach all nations. The Messiah does not begin his public work in Jerusalem, the city of God's name, but in a fishing village on the edge of the Gentile world—precisely where the prophet said the light would dawn. Darkness here is not merely night but spiritual blindness and the "shadow of death," the realm of judgment and despair; and against it stands a *great* light, the light that will be revealed fully when the Son rises from the dead.

---

#### III. The Proclamation: "Repent! For the Kingdom of Heaven Is at Hand" (v. 17)

From this time Jesus began to preach—and Matthew compresses his entire message into one sentence. Two words carry the weight.

*Repent* (Greek *metanoeite*) means a change of mind that issues in a change of direction: turn from sin and self-rule, turn toward God. It is the same word John the Baptist used (3:2), and it marks continuity between the forerunner and the Messiah. Repentance is not mere remorse; it is reorientation of the whole life before the King who has arrived.

*The Kingdom of Heaven is at hand.* Matthew's distinctive phrase—"kingdom of heaven" rather than "kingdom of God"—reflects a Jewish reverence that avoided speaking the divine name too readily. The *kērygma* here is that God's long-promised reign has broken into history: it is *at hand*, near, present in the person and work of Jesus himself. He teaches in the synagogues (v. 23), he heals the sick, he casts out demons—the kingdom is not an abstraction but a power visibly at work. The call to repentance makes sense only because the King has come: there is no longer any excuse for delay.

---

#### IV. The Calling of the First Disciples (vv. 18–22)

##### "Come After Me" (vv. 18–19)

Walking by the sea of Galilee, Jesus sees Simon Peter and Andrew casting their nets—they are fishermen, at their trade, in their element. And he calls them with words that define discipleship for all ages: *"Come after me, and I will make you fishers for men."*

"Come *after* me" (Greek *deute opiso mou*) is a picture of following behind a person, not joining an institution or enrolling in a program. Discipleship in Matthew's gospel is personal: it begins with a face-to-face call and consists of walking in the steps of Jesus. The promise that follows redefines their vocation. They will still fish, but now for people—drawing men out of the darkness described in Isaiah 9 into the light of the kingdom. The net becomes an image of the gospel's gathering work (cf. 13:47–50; 22:1–14).

Their response is immediate and costly: *they at once left their nets and followed him.* No deliberation, no conditions negotiated. What they left behind was livelihood, family expectation, and the security of the known. Matthew will later show that following Jesus can cost even more (10:37–39), but here the pattern is set: the call comes, and the disciple leaves everything at once.

##### James and John (vv. 20–22)

Going on from there, Jesus calls two other brothers, James and John, who are in the boat with their father Zebedee, mending their nets. They too leave immediately—but what they leave is not only the boat and the business; they leave *their father*. In a Jewish household the son's duty to his father was among the most sacred obligations, and Matthew does not soften the fact. The call of the kingdom outranks even the closest earthly ties—not in contempt for them, but because it binds the disciple to a higher loyalty (cf. 8:21–22; 10:37). Zebedee's household becomes, in effect, the first "church" behind the mission: the father will continue the trade while his sons follow the Son of God.

Notice also the method of the calling. Jesus does not send a letter or announce a vacancy; he walks by the sea, sees two ordinary men at work, and speaks their names into a new future. The gospel begins with particular people in particular places—fishermen on a Galilean shore—and from there reaches out to "all Syria" and beyond (v. 25).

---

#### V. A Summary of the Galilean Ministry (vv. 23–25)

The chapter closes with a panoramic sketch that will serve as the backdrop for much of what follows. Three activities characterize Jesus's work:

1. **Teaching in their synagogues**—he enters the established houses of Israel's worship and speaks with authority (cf. 7:28–29). He does not found a rival school; he fulfills and re-centers the Torah itself.
2. **Preaching the good news of the Kingdom**—the *euangelion*, the glad tidings that God's reign has arrived in him.
3. **Healing every disease and every sickness among the people**—a visible sign that the kingdom is not merely proclaimed but present, breaking the power of sin's consequences in body and spirit.

The report of him goes out through all Syria, and they bring to him *all* who are sick: those afflicted with various diseases and torments, demon-possessed, epileptics (the "moon-struck," whose seizures were often attributed to demonic influence), and paralytics. The list is deliberately comprehensive—every category of human misery finds its way to this man, and he heals them all. In the healing of demons as well as of bodies, Matthew shows that the kingdom's advance is a victory over the powers of darkness themselves; the same adversary repulsed in the wilderness (v. 11) is now driven out of his victims by the Son of God.

The final verse draws a map: great multitudes follow him from **Galilee**, the **Decapolis** (the Gentile cities to the east), **Jerusalem**, **Judea**, and **from beyond the Jordan**. From every quarter of the inhabited world, people come. Matthew's readers see the fulfillment of "Galilee of the Gentiles" already at work: the light that dawned in Zebulun and Naphtali is drawing the whole region—and, as the gospel will go on to show, the whole world—toward itself.

---

#### Conclusion

Matthew 4 presents the Messiah in his decisive hour. In the wilderness he passes the test that Israel failed, answering every assault of the tempter with the word of God and refusing every shortcut to the throne. He is the true Son who lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the Father, who does not test God but trusts him, and who worships no one but the Lord his God. From that victory flows everything else: the move into the land where Isaiah said the light would dawn, the proclamation that the Kingdom is at hand, the calling of men to leave all and follow him, and a ministry of teaching, preaching, and healing that draws multitudes from every region.

For the Christian reader, the chapter speaks on two levels. Historically, it tells us who Jesus is: the obedient Son, tested and victorious, now sending his light into the darkness of the world. Personally, it confronts us with the same questions the tempter asked Israel and asks us—What will you live by? Will you test God or trust him? Whom will you worship and serve? And it answers them not with argument but with a person: the one who walked out of the wilderness into Capernaum and said, *Come after me.*

### Chapter 5

#### I. The Setting: A New Moses, and More (vv. 1–2)

The scene opens with deliberate echoes of Sinai. As Moses went up into the mountain to receive the law for Israel, so Jesus "went up onto the mountain" to speak it. But the differences are as significant as the parallels. Moses climbed alone and returned with tablets; Jesus sits, and *his disciples come to him*. He is not the recipient of revelation but its source. The ancient rabbis said that when a master opened his mouth, grace flowed forth (cf. Ps. 78:2); Matthew records that "he opened his mouth and taught them." What follows — chapters 5–7 — is the charter of the Kingdom of Heaven, spoken by its King with an authority that leaves the crowds astonished (7:28–29).

#### II. The Beatitudes: The Citizens of the Kingdom (vv. 3–12)

The word "blessed" (*makarios*) does not describe happiness as a feeling but wholeness as a state — the condition of those who stand in God's favor. Notice what is blessed: not the rich, the powerful, the self-satisfied, but precisely those whom the world counts as losers.

- **The poor in spirit** (v. 3) are those who have abandoned every claim to self-sufficiency and come to God empty-handed, like the beggar in Isaiah 61:1. They alone can receive the Kingdom, because they do not try to earn it.
- **Those who mourn** (v. 4) grieve over sin — their own (Ps. 51) and the world's brokenness. Their comfort is promised as certainly as God's own presence (Isa. 61:2; 2 Cor. 1:3–4).
- **The gentle** (v. 5) are not the weak but the strong under control, the *anavim* of Psalm 37:11 who "inherit the earth." The world inherits the earth by the sword; the Kingdom's citizens inherit it by grace.
- **Those who hunger and thirst for righteousness** (v. 6) have a desperate appetite for God's justice to prevail, as in Isaiah 55:1. They shall be filled — not with their own projects, but with God himself.
- **The merciful** (v. 7) discover that mercy is not spent but multiplied; they "obtain mercy" because they have learned what it costs to forgive (cf. Mt. 18:33).
- **The pure in heart** (v. 8) are the single-hearted, whose devotion is undivided (Ps. 24:3–4). Their reward is the greatest of all: "they shall see God" — the face-to-face communion denied to Moses (Ex. 33:20) and promised to the redeemed (1 Thess. 1:9; Rev. 22:4).
- **The peacemakers** (v. 9) do not merely avoid quarrels but actively reconcile, as Christ reconciled us to the Father (2 Cor. 5:18–19). They are "called sons of God" because they bear the character of their Father.
- **Those persecuted for righteousness' sake** (v. 10) stand in the line of Israel's prophets and inherit what belongs to them: the Kingdom itself.

In verses 11–12 the address becomes personal — "Blessed are *you*" — and the persecution is now defined as being hated "for my sake." The disciple's suffering is grafted onto the prophets' and, ultimately, onto Christ's own. The command to "rejoice, and be exceedingly glad" is not stoicism but the joy of sharing in the Messiah's lot; the reward stored in heaven outweighs every present loss.

#### III. Salt and Light: The Church in the World (vv. 13–16)

Disciples are not to withdraw from the world but to permeate and illuminate it. **Salt** preserves against decay and gives flavor; Israel was called "the salt of the covenant" (Lev. 2:13). So the church is to hold society back from moral rot and season life with the taste of the Kingdom. The warning is severe: in that region salt was often dug up mixed with worthless minerals, so once it lost its savor it could not be restored — "good for nothing, but to be cast out." A faith that has lost its distinctiveness is a dead faith.

**A city on a hill cannot be hidden**, and no one lights a lamp only to bury it under a measuring basket; it goes on the stand so that *all in the house* may see by it. The purpose clause is essential: "that they may see your good works and glorify **your Father who is in heaven**." Christian witness is not self-promotion. When our light shines for God's glory, we are fulfilling our vocation; when it shines for ours, we have become the very darkness we were sent to displace.

#### IV. Fulfilling the Law (vv. 17–20)

Jesus forestalls two errors at once: that he has come to *abolish* Torah, and that his teaching will make it obsolete. He came "to fulfill" — to bring the law to its intended goal, to embody its meaning, and to interpret it with final authority. His claim to the permanence of Scripture is absolute: until heaven and earth pass away, not one **iota** (the smallest Hebrew letter) or one **dot** (the tiny pen-stroke on letters like *dalet*) shall vanish "until all is accomplished." The law stands until its purposes are fully realized — which happens in Christ.

The practical demand follows: whoever breaks even the least commandment *and teaches others to do so* will be called least in the Kingdom; whoever **does and teaches** will be called great. Orthodoxy without practice, and practice without teaching, both fall short. And the standard is set high: "unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will by no means enter." This is not a call to keep *more* rules but to obey *deeper* — from the heart rather than the surface, in love rather than self-justification (cf. Mt. 23).

#### V. The Six Antitheses: The Heart of the Law (vv. 21–48)

Six times Jesus takes a familiar saying — "You have heard that it was said" — and drives it to its root with "But I say to you." In each case he does not discard the commandment but reveals its full depth, reaching beneath outward acts to the intentions of the heart.

##### 1. Murder and Anger (vv. 21–26)
The sixth commandment forbids the act; Jesus reaches the soil from which it grows: unrighteous anger, contempt, dehumanizing speech. "Raca" was Aramaic for an empty-headed fool — a term of despising scorn; "you fool" (*moros*) is the word from which our "moron" descends. The escalating courts — the local judgment seat, the Sanhedrin ("the council"), and finally "the fire of Gehenna" (the valley outside Jerusalem where refuse and the bodies of executed criminals burned day and night) — picture a judgment that grows more certain as contempt hardens into hatred.

The two illustrations drive the point home. If you remember at the altar that your brother has something against you, **leave your gift** and be reconciled first: worship offered while carrying unresolved bitterness is hollow. And settle with your adversary "quickly, while you are with him on the way," lest the case harden into judgment — a picture drawn from Deuteronomy 19:16–21, where the false witness suffers the penalty he intended for another, down to "the last penny." The lesson is urgent: do not carry unforgiveness into God's presence, and seek reconciliation while there is still time.

##### 2. Adultery and Lust (vv. 27–30)
The seventh commandment governs the body; Jesus governs the eye and the heart. To "look at a woman with intent to lust" is already adultery in the only place where all adultery begins — the mind. The remedies are deliberate hyperbole: gouge out the eye, cut off the hand. No rabbi would have taken them literally; their force is radical. Better that one member perish than the whole body be cast into Gehenna. Whatever it costs — a habit, a relationship, a comfort — uproot what feeds your sin.

##### 3. Divorce (vv. 31–32)
Deuteronomy 24:1 permitted a certificate of divorce, and the rabbinic schools debated how lightly it might be used — one school allowing it for anything, even burnt dinner. Jesus restores marriage to its creation design (Gen. 2:24): a permanent union. Except on the ground of *porneia* (sexual immorality), putting away a wife "makes her an adulteress" — for in that society a divorced woman was socially compelled to remarry — and whoever marries her commits adultery. Far from being harsh, this teaching protects the vulnerable, especially women, from being treated as disposable.

##### 4. Oaths (vv. 33–37)
The law required vows to be kept (Lev. 19:12; Num. 30:2); Jesus forbids swearing altogether. Heaven is God's throne, the earth his footstool, Jerusalem the city of the great King — none of these are ours to invoke. And by your own head you cannot swear, for you cannot make one hair white or black. The cure for a culture of half-truths and evasions is not better oaths but integrity of word: "Let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No' be 'No.' Whatever is more than these is of the evil one" (cf. Jas. 5:12). A Christian's word should need no collateral.

##### 5. Retaliation (vv. 38–42)
"An eye for an eye" was, in its ancient setting, a *limit* on vengeance — but Jesus goes beyond even that. "Do not resist him who is evil" does not mean passive acceptance of all wrongdoing; it means refusing to be dragged into the cycle of retaliation that multiplies evil. The three examples are vivid:

- **The blow on the right cheek** was an insult delivered with the back of the hand. Turning the other cheek is not groveling but a dignified refusal to answer degradation with degradation — an invitation for the aggressor to see himself as he is.
- **The lawsuit over the coat**: the undergarment was a debtor's pledge that the law protected (Deut. 24:10–13). Letting the plaintiff take your outer cloak *also* is a deliberate, public protest against an inhumane system — suffering without sinning.
- **The forced mile** was the Roman *angareia*, conscription to carry a soldier's baggage. Going two miles turns oppression into witness and seizes back the initiative.

And the summary: give to the one who asks; do not turn away the borrower. The citizen of the Kingdom answers violence with generosity.

##### 6. Love of Enemies (vv. 43–48)
"You shall love your neighbor" is Leviticus 19:18; "hate your enemy" was not in Scripture but reflected a common rabbinic gloss. Jesus replaces the whole formula with its divine source: "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you... pray for those who persecute you — **that you may be children of your Father**." The ground of enemy-love is imitation. The Father "makes his sun rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the just and the unjust" — common grace that refuses to discriminate. If we love only those who love us, we are no better than tax collectors; if we greet only our friends, we do nothing more than everyone else.

The chapter closes with its summit: "Therefore you shall be **perfect**, just as your Father in heaven is perfect." *Teleios* here means complete, wholehearted, undivided — not sinless. The Father's love has no boundary line; neither should ours. This is the law of Moses fulfilled at last: love God and love everyone, without exception.

#### VI. Conclusion: The Character of the Kingdom

Read as a whole, Matthew 5 presents the life of those who already belong to the Kingdom. It begins with the poor in spirit receiving it, moves through the vocation of salt and light, sets the standard of a righteousness that reaches the heart, and ends in love without limits. Three things deserve final emphasis:

1. **Christ's authority.** Six times "But I say to you" stands where only God could stand. Jesus interprets Torah as its Author — a claim that points straight to his identity (cf. 7:28–29; John 5:39).
2. **Grace before law.** The Beatitudes are not a ladder to climb but a portrait of those who have come empty-handed to God. This demanding teaching is lived, not by willpower, but by the Spirit, who alone "fulfills the righteous requirement of the law" (Rom. 8:4).
3. **The shape of discipleship.** The disciple mourns and hopes, preserves and shines, reconciles before worshipping, uproots sin at the root, speaks with integrity, answers evil with generosity, and loves without exception — all for the glory of the Father in heaven.

Here is the mind of Christ set before us: not a religion of external compliance, but a transformation of the heart that makes its bearers like their heavenly Father. To follow this chapter is to follow him.

### Chapter 6

#### Introduction: The Heart of the Sermon

Matthew 6 stands at the center of the Sermon on the Mount. In chapter 5 Jesus has set the standard of the kingdom — purity of heart, mercy, peacemaking, love of enemies. Chapter 7 will test the hearers ("not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord'..."). Between them, chapter 6 turns the spotlight onto the three great pillars of Jewish religious life — **almsgiving, prayer, and fasting** — and then widens its lens to the deeper questions of treasure, loyalty, and trust.

The governing principle is stated in verse 1: *"Be careful that you don't do your charitable giving before men, to be seen by them, or else you have no reward from your Father who is in heaven."* The Greek word translated "charitable giving" (and "merciful deeds") is *dikaiosynē* — righteousness. Jesus is not condemning almsgiving, prayer, and fasting; he is purifying them. Every act of devotion can be either an offering to God or a performance for men. The difference is never in the outward form but in the inward motive. This chapter is, above all, a **mirror held up to the heart** — and like every mirror the Law holds up, its purpose is not merely to convict but to drive us to Christ, who alone kept it perfectly and bore its curse for us (Galatians 3:10–14).

---

#### 6:2–4 — Almsgiving in Secret

Almsgiving (*tzedakah*, righteousness) was counted among the most meritorious of works; the prophet Hosea commands, *"Love mercy, and do justice"* (Hosea 6:6). Jesus does not dispute this. His objection is to the **trumpet** — the public fanfare that turns a gift into an advertisement. The hypocrites of the synagogues and streets "have received their reward": applause, reputation, self-satisfaction. They are paid in full; nothing remains for heaven.

The famous instruction — *"don't let your left hand know what your right hand does"* — is deliberate hyperbole. It is not a command to deceive those in need or to hoard charity where it cannot help; rather, it pictures a generosity so absorbed in love that it is unaware of itself. The point is the contrast: the Father *"who sees in secret will reward you openly."* God's economy runs opposite to the world's. What is hidden from men is magnified by God.

Christ himself is the pattern here. He gave freely and constantly, yet he rebuked those who sought glory (John 5:44; 7:18). Paul later describes him as *"though he was rich, yet for your sake became poor, so that you through his poverty might become rich"* (2 Corinthians 8:9) — the ultimate gift given without a trumpet.

---

#### 6:5–8 — Prayer: The Inner Room and the Father's Knowledge

Prayer is next. The hypocrites *"love to stand and pray in the synagogues and in the corners of the streets"* — standing being the posture of formal prayer, chosen here for visibility (cf. the Pharisee in Luke 18:11–12). Jesus' alternative is strikingly domestic: *"enter into your inner room, and having shut your door."* The *tameid*, the private chamber, with its door closed against distraction and audience. Prayer, like almsgiving, is essentially a matter between the soul and God.

Then comes the warning against **vain repetitions** (*battalogia*, idle word-piling). Jesus has in view pagan practice: the heathen believed that by sheer volume of words they could wear down or compel indifferent gods — as the prophets of Baal *"cried until noon, calling, 'O Baal, answer us!'"* (1 Kings 18:28). But our God is a Father. *"Your Father knows what things you need before you ask him"* (cf. Psalm 139; 145:18). Prayer is not information we feed to the dark; it is communion with a Father who already knows — and who, in his wisdom, has appointed prayer as the channel through which he meets us.

*"Pray like this"* — Jesus does not hand over a magic formula but a **pattern**, a summary of all right praying: God-centered first, creaturely need second; corporate ("our," "us") throughout; ending in doxology.

##### The Lord's Prayer (6:9–13)

**"Our Father in heaven."** The opening word is revolutionary. In the synagogue the Jews prayed to "the Lord our God"; Jesus teaches his disciples to pray *Abba*. It is intimacy wrapped in awe — nearness ("Father") and transcendence ("in heaven"). And it is *our*: prayer belongs to a family, not to isolated individuals.

**"May your name be kept holy."** The first petition is for God, not for us. "Name" stands for his whole character and reputation; to hallow it is to treat it as holy — in our own lives and in the world (cf. Ezekiel 36:23). Every right prayer begins with God's glory, not our wants.

**"Let your Kingdom come."** The eschatological hope of Israel and the church: may God's reign break in fully, as it began in Christ's first coming and will be completed at his return. It is also a present petition — may he rule here and now through his Word and Spirit.

**"Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven."** In heaven the angels do his will without resistance (Psalm 103:20–21). We pray that earth would become like heaven — and, more immediately, that *we* would submit as they do. These are the very words of Christ himself in Gethsemane (Matthew 26:39), the prayer of the one who alone could say it perfectly.

**"Give us today our daily bread."** "Bread" stands for all bodily need; "daily" (*epiousios*) means "for this day," sufficient. The echo of the **manna** (Exodus 16) is hard to miss: a people learning, day by day, that their bread comes from heaven and not from their own anxious storing. It is a prayer against both greed and self-sufficiency.

**"Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors."** The metaphor is financial: sin is a debt we cannot pay (cf. Colossians 2:14, where the "certificate of debt" was nailed to the cross). But notice the clause — forgiveness received and forgiveness given are one posture of heart. We do not *earn* God's pardon by pardoning others; rather, an unforgiving heart proves that we have never truly grasped the magnitude of our own forgiveness (Matthew 18:23–35).

**"Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one."** God does not tempt (James 1:13); we ask to be led *past* testing beyond what we can bear, and to be rescued from the adversary who lies at the root of all such testing. It is the prayer of dependence — *"not my will, but yours"* — spoken by a people who know they cannot stand unaided.

**"For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen."** This doxology, found in the earliest manuscripts, closes the prayer as it opened: with God. All petitions are gathered up into worship. The "Amen" — *truly, so be it* — seals what the whole family of God means together.

##### 6:14–15 — The Condition of Forgiveness

*"If you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you."* This is not a transaction but a diagnosis. A heart that clings to its own injuries has not received the gospel; a heart washed by it cannot help washing others (1 John 1:9; Ephesians 4:32). The cross is where our debts were canceled — and the same mercy, once truly believed, overflows.

---

#### 6:16–18 — Fasting Without a Mask

Fasting was Israel's language of mourning and repentance (Jonah 3:5; Daniel 9:3). The hypocrites *disfigured* their faces — ashes, disheveled hair — so that piety could be read at a glance. Jesus inverts the sign: *"anoint your head and wash your face."* Look as you normally do — indeed, look like a person at peace with God. Fasting is not a costume for an audience; it is a quiet turning of the soul toward God, where only he sees and only he rewards. Isaiah 58's "fast I have chosen" (Isaiah 58:6–7) shows the same thing: true fasting issues in mercy, not in a performance of misery.

---

#### 6:19–21 — Treasures and the Eye

The scene shifts from piety to **possessions**. Earthly treasure is doubly insecure: it decays (moth and rust) and it is stolen. Heavenly treasure — stored by faithful giving, love, and obedience — cannot be touched by either. The logic of verse 21 is simple and devastating: *"where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."* The heart follows the wallet. Whatever we ultimately invest in, we will ultimately love.

The "eye" of verses 22–23 is a lamp for the whole body — that is, our vision determines whether we live in light or darkness. A *sound* (or "single") eye is one that is generous and undivided, fixed on God; an *evil* eye is one that is stingy, envious, and double-minded. The person who sees life through generosity walks in light; the person whose vision is narrowed by covetousness lives, as Jesus says with sobering force, in *"how great is the darkness!"*

---

#### 6:22–23 — No Divided Loyalty

*"No one can serve two masters."* Mammon — wealth personified as a rival lord — cannot share the throne with God. The choice is not between "some" money and "some" of God, but between two ultimate loyalties. This verse is the hinge of the chapter: having purified piety (almsgiving, prayer, fasting) and reoriented treasure, Jesus now addresses the anxiety that a mammon-trusting heart inevitably produces.

---

#### 6:24–34 — The Antidote to Anxiety

Anxiety (*merimna*) is the natural fruit of treating created things as uncreated security. Jesus answers it with two arguments and one command.

**The argument from value.** *"Isn't life more than food, and the body more than clothing?"* Consider the birds: they neither sow nor reap, yet the Father feeds them — and *"aren't you of much more value than they?"* If God cares for sparrows (Matthew 10:29), how much more for those who bear his image and are bought with his Son's blood?

**The argument from nature.** Consider the lilies: they neither toil nor spin, yet outdress Solomon. And this grass *"which today exists and tomorrow is thrown into the oven"* — the most transient of flowers — receives such clothing. If God so adorns what will not last, will he not much more clothe *you*? The gentle rebuke — *"you of little faith"* (*oligopistoi*) — is a father's voice, not a judge's: trust me, as I have trusted you.

**The command.** *"Seek first God's Kingdom and his righteousness; and all these things will be given to you as well."* This is not passivity but **ordered priority**: pursue God's reign and his right way of life — in worship, obedience, mission, holiness — and the Father who feeds the birds will provide for his children. The Gentiles *"seek after all these things"*; their whole civilization is organized around anxious provision. The people of God live under a different sky: a Father who knows what they need before they ask (6:8).

The chapter closes with one of the most humane sentences in Scripture: *"Don't be anxious for tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Each day's own evil is sufficient."* Faith is not the denial that trouble comes — each day brings its own — but the refusal to pre-borrow tomorrow's grief today. One day at a time, under the Father's care, is how his children walk.

---

#### Conclusion: The Chapter in Light of the Cross

Read as a whole, Matthew 6 is both a mirror and a pointer. As a **mirror**, it exposes what we already know to be true: we do give to be seen, we pray for an audience, we fast for applause, we store our treasure on earth, we serve mammon in secret, and we live in anxiety because we have made creatures our security. No one reads this chapter without being convicted; that is its purpose.

But the Sermon on the Mount was not given to be kept by human effort. It was given to show us **Christ** — the only man who did his righteousness for the Father's pleasure alone, who withdrew to pray in secret (Luke 5:16), who fasted forty days (Matthew 4:2), who owned no treasure on earth (Hebrews 13:5; 2 Corinthians 8:9), and who in Gethsemane prayed the very words of this chapter — *"not my will, but yours"* — and drank the cup to the dregs. On the cross he canceled our debt (Colossians 2:14); by his resurrection he opened the kingdom; by his Spirit he teaches us to pray, "Our Father."

So the Christian's response to Matthew 6 is not self-improvement but **union with Christ**: receiving his perfect righteousness as a gift, praying the Lord's Prayer as members of his family through him, and walking daily — one day at a time — in the confidence that the Father who fed the birds and clothed the lilies has counted us of far more value, because he gave his only Son for us.

*To God alone be the glory, now and forever. Amen.*

### Chapter 7

#### Introduction: The Summit of the Sermon

Matthew 7 closes the Sermon on the Mount (5–7), the great manifesto of the Kingdom that Jesus delivered from a hillside in Galilee. If chapters 5 and 6 established *what* the righteousness of the Kingdom looks like—its purity, its piety, its trust in the Father—chapter 7 turns to *how* disciples will live it out: in their relationships with one another, in their prayer, in their discernment, and ultimately in their response to Christ himself. The chapter moves from the intimate (a brother's eye) to the cosmic (the final judgment), and it ends with a scene that echoes Sinai: the people stand astonished before a Teacher who speaks not as an interpreter of Moses but as the Lord of Moses.

The structure is deliberate. Jesus addresses three postures toward God—**judgment, petition, and obedience**—and warns against two deceptions: **false teachers** and **self-deception**. The whole chapter converges on one question, answered in verses 21–27: *What does it mean to truly know the Lord?*

---

#### 1. "Judge Not" (vv. 1–5)

> *"Don't judge, so that you won't be judged."*

The chapter opens with a warning that is often quoted but rarely understood. The Greek of verse 1 (*krinate*) does not forbid all discernment—Jesus himself commands judgment in verses 16 and 20 ("by their fruits you will know them"). What he forbids is **condemning, self-righteous judgment**: the posture of standing over another person as a prosecutor while harboring the same sin oneself.

The logic of verse 2 is not a mechanical "karma" but a statement about the court in which we all stand. We are not exempt from God's tribunal; we are participants in it. To wield a harsh measure against others is to set the standard by which the Judge will weigh us (cf. Romans 2:1–3, where Paul argues that "the judgment of God is just upon those who practice such things"). The measure you use becomes the measure you invite.

The illustration of the **speck and the beam** (vv. 3–5) is deliberately grotesque. A *mote* (a splinter of dust) in a brother's eye, while a *beam* (a timber or rafter) blinding one's own? The point is not that we are blind to our own sin—though we are—but that **hypocrisy is the refusal to see it**. The word "hypocrite" here carries its theatrical sense: an actor wearing a mask, performing holiness while concealing corruption.

Notice the order of remedy in verse 5: *first* remove the beam, *then* you will see clearly to help your brother. Christian correction of others is not abolished; it is **reordered**. It must flow from repentance, not from superiority. The church that polices its members while indulging its own pride has inverted the sequence Jesus commands.

**Application:** Before we speak a word of correction, we must be on our knees in confession. Grace received is the only credential for grace given.

---

#### 2. "Do Not Give What Is Holy to Dogs" (v. 6)

This verse sits awkwardly beside the one before it and has puzzled readers for centuries. The most natural reading, in context, is about **discernment in sharing the gospel and its privileges**. "Holy things" (*hagia*) refers to what belongs to God—the table of grace, the mysteries of the Kingdom (cf. 1 Cor. 10:14, 21). Dogs and swine were unclean animals that would not value bread or pearls but would trample it and turn on the giver.

Jesus is not teaching contempt for people—his very next words are "Ask, and it will be given you," addressed to all who come. Rather, he warns against **indiscriminate exposure of sacred things to settled, mocking hostility**. There is a time to proclaim openly and a wisdom in not scattering the treasure of the gospel where it will only be mocked and its bearers harmed (cf. 10:16, "be wise as serpents"). The pearl must not become a pebble in the mouth of a swine.

**Application:** Evangelism is both urgent and discerning. We press the gospel on all, but we do not naively expose the heart of Christ to those whose only purpose is to devour it—and we guard our own hearts from becoming such scorers.

---

#### 3. "Ask, Seek, Knock" (vv. 7–12)

> *"Ask, and it will be given you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened."*

Here the tone shifts abruptly from warning to invitation. The three imperatives are not a formula for manipulating God but a portrait of **persistent, childlike dependence**. To *ask* is to confess need; to *seek* is to pursue with effort; to *knock* is to stand at the door when the answer is delayed. The repetition in verse 7 ("everyone who asks receives") is emphatic: no sincere petitioner is turned away from the Father's door.

The illustration of verses 8–11 is one of the most tender in Scripture. Jesus does not argue from God's power but from **fatherhood**. No human father—*even an evil one*, he says, and that is a staggering concession about fallen humanity—would hand his child a stone when it asks for bread, or a serpent when it asks for fish. If broken, selfish men give good gifts to their children, "how much more" (*posō mallon*) will the Father in heaven give good things to those who ask? The argument from lesser to greater rests on one premise: **God is not merely a provider; he is a Father** (cf. 1 John 3:1).

Two notes of caution. First, "good things" does not mean every request will be granted as asked; the Father gives what is truly good, which may differ from what we desire (James 1:5; 1 John 5:14). Second, the promise is to *those who ask*—prayer is the channel of blessing, and a life without asking is a life that has stopped trusting.

##### The Golden Rule (v. 12)

> *"Therefore, whatever you desire for men to do to you, you shall also do to them; for this is the law and the prophets."*

The "therefore" is significant: the Golden Rule flows from the Father's generosity. We treat others as we wish to be treated because *we ourselves have been asked of a Father who gives good things*. Jesus calls this summary "the Law and the Prophets"—not an addition to the Torah but its heart (cf. Lev. 19:18; Deut. 10:19). Note that it is a rule of **positive obligation**, not a license: it commands us to do good, not merely to refrain from harm.

**Application:** Prayer and ethics are one movement. The man who kneels asking the Father for mercy will walk differently with his neighbor than the man who has never asked.

---

#### 4. "Enter by the Narrow Gate" (vv. 13–14)

Jesus now steps back to show the two roads of human history. The **wide gate and broad way** leads to *apoleia*—destruction, ruin, perdition—and it is crowded: "many take it." The **narrow gate and restricted way** leads to *zōē*, life (the life of the Kingdom), and "few find it."

The contrast is not primarily about difficulty but about **conformity**. The broad way is the road of self-rule: doing what feels right, following the crowd, avoiding offense. The narrow gate is the entrance that requires surrender—repentance, faith in Christ, and the daily dying to self that Jesus has just described throughout the Sermon (5:3–16; 6:19–33). It is "narrow" because it admits only those who come as they are, broken and believing, not those who arrive with their own righteousness.

The sobering statistics—"many" on one road, "few" on the other—should humble every comfortable Christian. The crowd at the foot of the hill (v. 28) is large; the gate is small. **Application:** Do not measure your standing by the size of the assembly. Measure it by whether you have entered through the door of repentance and faith, and are walking the way of obedience.

---

#### 5. "Beware of False Prophets" (vv. 15–20)

> *"By their fruits you will know them."*

The warning is urgent because the danger is internal: false prophets come **"in sheep's clothing."** They do not announce themselves as wolves; they look like the flock, speak the language of the flock, and move among the flock. The deception is external conformity covering an inward "ravening" nature (the Greek *harpages* suggests a wolf that seizes and tears).

The test Jesus gives is **fruit**, and he states it twice (vv. 16, 20) for emphasis. The imagery of trees and fruit (cf. Luke 6:43–45; John 15:1–8) teaches three things:

1. **Fruit flows from nature.** A good tree cannot produce bad fruit, nor a corrupt tree good fruit. Character is not performed; it is produced. What a person *consistently bears*—in speech, in relationships, in the handling of money and power—reveals what they *are*.
2. **The test is sustained, not isolated.** One good deed or one scandal does not settle the question; the whole harvest counts.
3. **There is a final pruning.** "Every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire" (v. 19). Fruitlessness is not merely disappointing; it is fatal.

**Application:** Evaluate teachers by their long-term fruit—do they produce holiness, humility, love, and faithfulness to Scripture? And evaluate yourself by the same standard. The question "by their fruits you will know them" is a mirror as much as a magnifying glass.

---

#### 6. "Lord, Lord" (vv. 21–23)

> *"Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father."*

This is the hinge of the chapter and of the whole Sermon. The phrase "Lord, Lord" (the doubled vocative signals intimacy and urgency) was the confession of genuine believers—yet it is not sufficient. **Profession without obedience is not discipleship.** The criterion for entering the Kingdom is doing *the will of the Father*—which, in the context of this sermon, means the very things Jesus has just commanded: humility, purity, mercy, trust, and love.

The scene of verse 23 is chilling. On the last day, a crowd stands before Christ claiming impressive religious credentials: prophecy, exorcism, mighty works—all done "in your name." And the answer is not "your works were counterfeit" but **"I never knew you"** (*oudepote egnōn hymas*). The tragedy is not that their works failed; it is that **they were strangers to the Lord whose name they bore**. They had religion without relationship, activity without abiding (cf. John 15:4–6).

"Depart from me, you workers of lawlessness" (*hoperontes tēn anomian*)—the final verdict names their true character. The works were real; the workers were not his. This is why verses 21 and 23 must be read together: **the will of the Father is done by those whom Christ knows, and those whom Christ knows are known by their obedience.**

**Application:** Examine whether your faith is a relationship or a résumé. The question is not "What have I done in God's name?" but "Does he know me—and do I know him?"

---

#### 7. The Two Builders (vv. 24–27)

The Sermon closes with the parable of the two houses, which functions as its summary and its seal. Both builders **hear** the same words; both build houses; both face the same storm—rain, floods, winds beating against the walls. The only difference is the **foundation**: rock or sand. And the outcome is total: one house stands, the other falls "with a great fall."

The rock is not effort, nor moralism, nor even hearing itself—for the foolish man heard too. The rock is **doing the words of Jesus**: obedience grounded in him. The sand is everything else—feeling, tradition, cultural religion, self-approval—soil that looks solid until the first real pressure comes.

Notice what the storm tests: not the roof (outward appearance) but the foundation (inward reality). The day of trial—suffering, loss, temptation, death—is when foundations are exposed. The wise man's house did not avoid the storm; it **survived** it. Christian life is not exemption from trials but endurance through them, because our obedience is built on Christ rather than on ourselves.

This parable also answers the objection that Jesus' teaching is too demanding. He does not say, "These words are hard, so you may ignore them." He says: hear them and *do* them, or your house will fall when the rain comes. There is no third option—no house built on half a rock.

**Application:** Ask yourself where your life is founded. When the storms come—and they will—what will hold? Only the doing of Christ's words, resting in Christ himself, who is the true Rock (1 Pet. 2:4–8).

---

#### 8. The Multitudes Astonished (v. 28)

> *"And when Jesus had finished these sayings, the multitudes were astonished at his teaching, for he was teaching them as one having authority, and not as their scribes."*

Matthew's closing note echoes his opening portrait of the Teacher (7:29). The scribes taught by citation—authority borrowed from Moses and the rabbis. Jesus taught with **intrinsic authority**: "But I say to you" (5:21–48). He did not interpret the Law from a distance; he spoke as its Lord, binding heaven and earth in his own word. The crowds' astonishment is the proper response to Scripture itself—awe before a voice that is not merely human.

It is worth pausing here. The Sermon on the Mount was not delivered by a rabbi explaining tradition. It was delivered by **the Word made flesh** (John 1:14), the one who would say, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (28:18). To read Matthew 7 as moral advice is to miss the point of verse 28: these words carry the weight of their Author.

---

#### Summary Themes and Final Application

Matthew 7 can be gathered into five movements, each with a lasting claim on the believer:

1. **Humility before others** (vv. 1–6): Remove your own beam; share holy things with discernment.
2. **Confidence in the Father** (vv. 7–12): Ask, seek, knock—and treat neighbors as you wish to be treated.
3. **The two ways** (vv. 13–14): The broad road of self is crowded; the narrow way of Christ leads to life.
4. **Discernment and self-examination** (vv. 15–23): Judge teachers by fruit, and judge yourself by whether Christ truly knows you.
5. **Obedience as foundation** (vv. 24–28): Hear the words of Jesus and *do* them, or face a fall that no amount of religious activity can prevent.

The chapter's final word is not comfort but **authority**—and in that authority lies both terror and hope. Terror, because no profession, work, or title stands before the Judge who says "I never knew you." Hope, because the same Jesus who pronounces that verdict is the Father who gives good things to those who ask, the narrow gate that admits the humble, and the rock on which a house can stand through every storm. The Sermon ends where it began: with a call to come to Christ, hear his words, and do them—trusting not in our own foundation, but in his.

*Soli Deo Gloria.*

### Chapter 8

#### Introduction: Down from the Mountain

Matthew has just presented the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7), and verse 1 picks up with a deliberate echo of Moses: "When he came down from the mountain, great multitudes followed him." Just as Israel watched Moses descend Sinai with the tablets in his hand (Exodus 32:6; 34:29), so the crowds now watch Jesus descend with the words of the kingdom on his lips. But Matthew's point is that this new Moses does not merely speak — he acts. Chapter 8 is a catalogue of miracles, and it is arranged to display three domains of authority: over **disease** (the leper, the fever, the sick), over **demons** (the evening exorcisms, the Gergesenes), and over **creation itself** (the storm). The chapter answers the question that will finally be asked in 16:15 — "Who do you say that I am?" — not with a speech but with deeds.

---

#### 1. The Cleansing of the Leper (8:1–4)

Leprosy (*tsara'at*) was far more than a skin disease in Israel's world. Under Leviticus 13–14 it rendered a person ceremonially unclean, and under Leviticus 13:45–46 the sufferer was driven from camp, crying "Unclean! Unclean!" The leper who approaches Jesus therefore represents everything the kingdom of God has come to restore: the outcast, the defiled, the one with no standing before priest or community.

Note the leper's words: "Lord, **if you will**, you can make me clean." This is not flattery but genuine humility. The man does not presume upon Jesus; he acknowledges that healing depends on divine will and grace. Compare the woman with the issue of blood in 9:21–22 — the same posture of trusting surrender.

Two details are striking. First, **Jesus touches him**. A ritually observant Jew would have been defiled by contact with a leper; here Jesus deliberately crosses the boundary of purity law to identify with the outcast. Grace in Matthew's Gospel is never distant — it reaches out and touches. Second, Jesus' reply, "**I will; be made clean**," is a divine self-determination. The Greek *ethelō* ("I will") echoes God's own initiative throughout the Old Testament. The leper's "if you will" is answered by Christ's "I will."

The command that follows is often puzzling: "See that you tell nobody; but go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift that Moses commanded, as a testimony to them." This is not contradiction. Jesus keeps the **Messianic secret** (he does not want his ministry reduced to a spectacle of wonders), yet he also upholds the Law — consistent with 5:17. The healed man must go to the priest for the formal examination and the offerings prescribed in Leviticus 14:10, so that the healing would be publicly attested "as a testimony to them." Jesus does not abolish Moses; he fulfills him. And of course the leper disobeys the silence command (Mark 1:45) — which only proves how irresistible the news of grace is.

*Application:* Whatever our "leprosy" — sin, shame, exclusion — Christ touches what no one else will touch, and his word alone makes clean. And like the leper, we are sent to testify, not in secret, but openly.

---

#### 2. The Faith of the Centurion (8:5–13)

In Capernaum a Roman centurion — an officer of the occupying army, a Gentile, and therefore outside the covenant community — comes with a request for his servant. Jesus' immediate reply, "I will come and heal him," shows his readiness to cross every social and religious boundary.

The centurion's answer is one of the most remarkable expressions of faith in all Scripture:

> "Lord, I'm not worthy for you to come under my roof. Just say the word, and my servant will be healed."

His argument rests on **authority**. He knows himself to be a man under authority (to Rome) who exercises authority over others (his soldiers). His word moves men because it carries the weight of his office. From this he draws a breathtaking conclusion: if *his* mere word, backed by delegated authority, is sufficient for his soldiers, then the word of Jesus — whose authority is not delegated but inherent — is sufficient to heal at a distance. The centurion has perceived what Israel often failed to grasp: that **the word of Jesus carries creative power**, as God's word did in creation (Psalm 33:9). He does not need Jesus' presence; he needs only Jesus' word.

Jesus "marveled" — a rare word for him in Matthew — and declares, "I haven't found so great a faith, not even in Israel." This is a stunning verdict: the greatest faith he has encountered is not in the covenant people but in a Gentile soldier. It foreshadows the mission to the nations (10:5–6; 21:31–32) and the reversal that will come at Pentecost.

The warning that follows is severe. "Many will come from the east and the west, and will sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the Kingdom of Heaven" — an image of the eschatological banquet (cf. Isaiah 25:6–8; Luke 14:15). But "the children of the Kingdom will be thrown out into the outer darkness. There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth." Covenant membership by birth is not salvation. Those who are *inside* — the "children" — can still be cast out, just as those from east and west can come in. This is Matthew's standing warning against presumption (cf. 7:21–23; 13:41; 22:13; 25:30).

The chapter closes the pericope with a quiet verse of great comfort: "Go your way. Let it be done for you **as you have believed**. His servant was healed in that hour." Faith is not rewarded by a performance; it is answered by a word.

---

#### 3. Peter's Mother-in-Law and the Evening Heals (8:14–17)

Jesus enters Peter's house — the first glimpse of domestic life in the Gospel — and heals his wife's mother of a fever. The detail that "she got up and **served** him" is not incidental. Restoration in the kingdom is expressed in service. Wholeness received becomes wholeness given. This is the pattern of discipleship: we are healed so that we may serve.

When evening comes, the whole spiritual dimension of human affliction surfaces: "they brought to him many possessed with demons. He cast out the spirits **with a word**." The phrase echoes the centurion's "just say the word" — Jesus' spoken word is as effective over demons as over disease.

Then Matthew does something distinctive: he quotes **Isaiah 53:4**, "He took our infirmities and bore our diseases." This is a verse from the Suffering Servant song, and Matthew will return to it at the crucifixion (12:18–21; 27:29–30). The point is profoundly Christological. Healing in Jesus is not merely a technical miracle; it is an **atoning work**. In his person he takes up and bears the consequences of sin — sickness, suffering, death — that belong to us. Every healing in this chapter is a foretaste of the cross, where "he was wounded for our transgressions... and by his stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5). The kingdom's power over disease is ultimately the power of the coming suffering Servant.

---

#### 4. The Cost of Following (8:18–22)

"Now when Jesus saw great multitudes around him, he gave the order to depart to the other side." The crowd has become a barrier; Jesus must move on — and the "other side" is Gentile territory, which prepares us for the Gergesenes. In between, two men test what following him actually costs.

**The scribe** (8:18–19): "Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go." It sounds noble, but it is a romantic offer — following as long as the road is interesting. Jesus answers with an image of radical dependence: "The foxes have holes and the birds of the sky have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head." To follow him is to renounce self-sufficiency. The disciple's security is not a house or a plan but God's providence alone (cf. 6:25–34). Notice also the title "Son of Man" — the exalted figure of Daniel 7:13–14, who here is homeless and dependent. Majesty and humility are woven together in Christ.

**The other disciple** (8:21–22): "Lord, allow me first to go and bury my father." Whether this means a literal request for delay (rabbinic custom allowed postponement of study until one's parents were buried) or an idiom meaning "until my father dies," the request is the same: *let me finish my obligations first*. Jesus' reply is among the hardest in Scripture: "Follow me, and leave the dead to bury their own dead." The urgency of the kingdom admits no negotiation. Those who are alive to Christ must not be detained by the affairs of those who are not — and there may be a double meaning here: let the spiritually dead attend to the physically dead. Grace does not wait for our priorities to be convenient; it calls us out of them.

Both sayings together define discipleship in Matthew: **total dependence on Christ, and total priority given to him.**

---

#### 5. The Storm Calmed (8:23–27)

"When he got into a boat, his disciples followed him" — the simple pattern of the call repeated. Then "a violent storm came up on the sea." The Sea of Galilee was notorious for sudden, devastating squalls, and in the Old Testament the sea is the standing symbol of chaos and evil (Psalm 74:13–14; 89:9–10; Job 38:11). So this storm is not merely a weather event; it is the world's chaos breaking loose around the boat of the kingdom.

And Jesus — **asleep**. The evangelist does not explain it, but the picture is deliberate: while the disciples fight for their lives, the Lord sleeps in peace. His rest is not indifference; it is the composure of one for whom the sea has no ultimate authority.

The cry of the disciples is both desperate and, astonishingly, faithful: "Save us, Lord! We are dying!" They call him *Lord* (*Despotē*) — they turn to him in the storm rather than away from him. Their failure is not unbelief but **little faith**: fear that God's power has limits. Jesus' rebuke, "Why are you fearful, O you of little faith?" (the same words as in 6:30), echoes Israel's terror at the Red Sea (Exodus 14) and the psalmists' cries on the waters (Psalm 107:28–29).

Then he "got up, rebuked the wind and the sea, and there was a great calm." The language is that of **command over creation**. God stills the sea in Psalm 89:9; here Jesus does it. The result is *hēgemonia* — a profound, ordered stillness, like the silence after the creative word. The disciples' question — "What kind of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" — is the chapter's Christological hinge. The answer Matthew intends is not "a very good man" but *the Lord of creation*. Only God commands the sea; therefore the one who commands the sea shares in God's own authority.

*Application:* Every believer knows storms — of circumstance, of conscience, of doubt. The promise of this passage is that Christ is in the boat, and his word can still what our efforts cannot move. Fear is not the opposite of faith; it is its failure.

---

#### 6. The Demoniacs of Gergesa (8:28–34)

Jesus lands "in the country of the Gergesenes" (a variant of Mark's Gadarenes and Luke's Gerasenes), on the Gentile side of the lake, where the cliffs are riddled with tombs. Here meet him "two people possessed by demons... exceedingly fierce, so that nobody could pass that way." (Mark and Luke record one; Matthew's two may reflect a different tradition or a literary emphasis on the fullness of evil's power.)

Their first words are a confession: "**What do we have to do with you, Jesus, Son of God?**" Even the powers of darkness know who he is — and they know it as an accusation. "Have you come here to torment us before the time?" Evil perceives that its day is numbered; the arrival of the Son of God means judgment is coming, and these demons sense that their "time" may already be at hand. The title "Son of God," which Matthew will build toward in 14:6–7 and 26:63–68, is here confessed first by demons — a grim reminder that knowledge of who Christ is means nothing without submission to him.

The demons beg to be sent into the herd of pigs. Pigs were unclean animals (Leviticus 11:7) and a valuable economic asset; a large herd represented enormous wealth. Evil, expelled from men, seeks any host — even an unclean one. Jesus simply says, "Go!" He does not negotiate with demons; he commands them. And the whole herd "rushed down the cliff into the sea and died." The image is of chaos destroyed: evil, given its head, destroys itself. But note the cost — the swineherds flee, and the community loses great wealth. Deliverance from evil is real, but it is not free; the kingdom's advance disrupts economies built on compromise.

The ending is one of the most sobering in the Gospels. "All the city came out to meet Jesus. When they saw him, **they begged that he would depart from their borders**." The people who had been oppressed by the demoniacs — "nobody could pass that way" — do not thank their deliverer; they drive him away. They preferred the familiar tyranny of evil to the unsettling power of God. Compare the centurion in verses 5–13, a Gentile who welcomed Jesus' word with faith: here is a whole Gentile city that rejects him. The gospel goes out to all — but it divides. It will be received by some and expelled by others, and Matthew does not soften this truth.

*Application:* Deliverance from the power of sin and evil is genuine, immediate, and sometimes costly — to us and to our surroundings. And we should not be surprised when the world, even a world that benefits from the gospel, asks Christ to leave its borders. The disciple's faithfulness does not depend on the city's welcome.

---

#### Conclusion: The King in Action

Read as a whole, Matthew 8 is a portrait of the king at work. After the words of the Sermon come the works of the Messiah: he cleanses the leper, heals by word alone, bears our diseases as the Suffering Servant, commands the storm, and drives out the powers of darkness. The chapter presents three responses to him, and they map the whole of the Gospel:

1. **Faith that receives** — the centurion, who needed only a word;
2. **Discipleship that costs** — the call to leave everything, even comfortable obligations, and follow;
3. **Rejection that persists** — the city of Gergesa, which begged him to go away.

And running beneath all three is the question the disciples could not stop asking: "What kind of man is this?" The answer Matthew gives by the end of the chapter is clear enough for the reader, if not yet for the disciples: he is the Son of God, Lord of disease and demons and sea — the one in whom Isaiah's Servant has come to take up our infirmities, and in whom the kingdom of heaven has broken into a world that is half eager, half afraid, and wholly in need.

### Chapter 9

#### Introduction

Matthew 9 stands at a hinge in the Gospel. After the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7) and its first enactment in chapter 8, Jesus returns to Capernaum — "his own city," the base of his Galilean ministry (4:13). What follows is a mosaic of encounters: a paralytic lowered through a roof, a tax collector called by name, a question about fasting, a dying girl, a bleeding woman, two blind men, and a mute demoniac. Read together, these scenes form a portrait of the Messiah in his fullness — one who forgives sins, welcomes sinners, redefines piety around his person, conquers death itself, and moves with compassion toward a people "harassed and scattered." The chapter is, in effect, a compressed summary of the whole Gospel: grace to the broken, authority over every power that binds them.

#### 1. Forgiveness Before Healing (9:1–8)

The paralytic is brought on his bed, and Jesus' first words are not to the body but to the soul: "Son, cheer up! Your sins are forgiven you." The Greek word (*tharsei*) is a pastoral one — take courage, be of good comfort — and it will sound again in this chapter over the bleeding woman (9:22). Notice also *whose* faith Jesus sees: not primarily the paralytic's, but that of those who carried him. Faith here is communal; the friends who bore their neighbor to Christ are honored as if they had borne him themselves.

The scribes' reaction — "This man blasphemes" — is understandable within its own frame. In Jewish understanding, forgiveness of sins belongs to God alone (Ps. 103:2–3; Isa. 43:25). To pronounce it on one's own authority was to claim divine prerogative. Jesus, knowing their thoughts (a quiet assertion of omniscience, cf. Ps. 139:2–4), turns their objection into a demonstration. "Which is easier?" he asks. Rhetorically, saying "your sins are forgiven" is the *easier* utterance — invisible and unverifiable — while commanding a paralytic to walk is the bolder claim, because it can be tested in public. Jesus therefore performs the visible miracle as a seal upon the invisible pardon: if he can do what only God can do in the body, how much more does his word carry weight over sin?

The healing is not an end in itself but evidence of a greater authority — "that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins." The phrase "on earth" is striking: this is not merely a heavenly prerogative exercised from a distance, but an authority actively at work here and now. The crowd's response is telling: they glorify God "who had given such authority to men." Matthew leaves the question deliberately open for his readers — *what* does it mean that God has given such authority? The answer unfolds across the whole Gospel and reaches its climax in the confession of Peter (16:16) and the resurrection.

A word of balance is needed: this text must not be read as teaching that every sickness is the penalty for personal sin (cf. John 9:1–3). Rather, it shows that in Christ the deepest need — forgiveness — and the most urgent need — healing — are met together, because both flow from the same divine compassion (Ps. 103:3 joins the two: "who forgives all your iniquities, who heals all your diseases").

#### 2. The Calling of Matthew (9:9–13)

"Behold, a man called Matthew sitting at the tax collection office." Under Roman occupation, publican was a word for collaborator and extortioner; such men were classed with sinners and excluded from the pious table. Jesus' summons — "Follow me" — is startling in its simplicity. There is no program, no probation, no preliminary reform. Grace calls by name and changes everything at once.

The scandal follows immediately: Matthew hosts a banquet, and "many tax collectors and sinners came and sat down with Jesus." In Jewish practice, eating together was an act of covenantal acceptance; to share a table was to declare, "This person belongs to my people." The Pharisees' question — "Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?" — is really a question about the boundaries of holiness.

Jesus answers on two levels. First, by metaphor: "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick do." He does not come to certify the self-sufficient; he comes as the Physician to the diseased. Second, by Scripture, citing Hosea 6:6: "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice." The prophets had long warned that ritual without compassion misses the heart of God's will (cf. Isa. 1:11–17). And then comes one of the clearest mission statements in the New Testament: "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance."

The irony and glory of this passage is that the very man called from the tax office would go on to write this Gospel — a book that opens with Jesus' genealogy and closes with his ascension. The despised collector becomes the apostle who proclaims Christ as Immanuel, God with us. Grace does not merely forgive Matthew; it transforms him into a witness for generations.

#### 3. Fasting and the New Covenant (9:14–17)

John's disciples and the Pharisees ask why Jesus' followers do not fast. Fasting was a mark of piety, penitence, and dependence on God; to omit it seemed reckless. Jesus' answer reframes the entire question: "Can the friends of the bridegroom mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them?" He identifies himself as the bridegroom — an image drawn from the wedding songs of Israel (Ps. 45; Isa. 61:5–10), where God's coming kingdom is pictured as a wedding feast. The messianic age has arrived *in person*, and its first fruit is joy, not mourning.

Yet Jesus does not abolish fasting: "the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them, and then they will fast." This veiled reference to the cross (cf. John 3:14; 12:32) shows that his ministry includes suffering as well as joy, and the early church did indeed fast (Acts 13:2–3; 14:23).

The two parables — unshrunk cloth on an old garment, new wine in old wineskins — are not a rejection of the Old Covenant as such. The Law is holy, and Jesus will say so himself (5:17). Rather, they declare that the *new reality* inaugurated by Christ cannot be contained within the old forms. New wine ferments and expands; rigid skins burst. The new creation needs new vessels — the church, the new covenant community shaped by grace rather than by boundary-maintenance. Christianity, in other words, is not Judaism plus Jesus; it is a new order centered on the person of Christ.

#### 4. Two Healings: Faith That Touches (9:18–26)

A ruler — named Jairus in Mark and Luke — falls down and worships Jesus: "My daughter has just died, but come and lay your hand on her, and she will live." Matthew's phrasing suggests the child is already dead; if so, this is a resurrection before the tomb of Lazarus. Jesus rises and follows him, interrupting his teaching for a dying girl — an implicit ranking of priorities.

On the way, a woman who had suffered a discharge of blood for twelve years touches the fringe of his garment. Under Leviticus 15 such a condition rendered her ceremonially unclean; for twelve years she has lived on the margins of community, and (as Luke adds) spent all she had on physicians who failed her. Her reasoning is remarkable: "If I just touch *his* garment, I will be made well." She touches the tassel — the symbol of covenant obedience (Num. 15:38) — and is healed by the one who fulfills the covenant. In Mark's fuller account, power goes out from Jesus like a current; he does not lose it but *gives* it.

Jesus stops his journey to honor her. "Daughter, cheer up! Your faith has made you well." Faith here is not a work that earns healing but the open hand that receives it; yet Christ honors faith as the point of contact with grace, and he restores her publicly — daughterhood, wholeness, and dignity in one word.

The two stories are woven together so that while the woman is healed, the girl dies (in Mark and Luke) — and Jesus' word reaches both. He takes her by the hand, and "the girl arose." His declaration that she is "not dead, but sleeping" scandalizes the mourners with their flute players, yet it points beyond this hour to the resurrection: for those who are in Christ, death is not the final word (cf. John 11:11–14; 1 Thess. 4:13–15). The report "went out into all that land" — the quiet announcement of a new age.

#### 5. The Blind Men and the Mute Demoniac (9:27–34)

Two blind men cry out, "Have mercy on us, son of David!" This is a messianic title drawn from the covenant promise to David (2 Sam. 7; Ps. 89). They appeal not merely to Jesus' power but to his identity. His question — "Do you believe that I am able to do this?" — directs their faith to his person, and his answer, "According to your faith be it done to you," is not a magic formula but the gracious response of the Messiah to trusting hearts.

Jesus then commands silence — the so-called Messianic secret (cf. 8:4; 17:9) — yet the men "went out and spread abroad his fame in all that land." Matthew quietly shows that the secret cannot be kept: the light of Christ will outshine every attempt at concealment, and the Gospel must go public.

The mute demoniac receives a double deliverance — from the power of darkness and from physical muteness. The crowds' cry, "Nothing like this has ever been seen in Israel," echoes Isaiah's prophecy of the Messiah: "then the eyes of the blind shall be opened… and the tongue of the mute shall sing for joy" (Isa. 35:5–6; cf. 61:1). They recognize fulfillment even if they cannot yet name it fully.

Against this stands the Pharisees' counter-interpretation: "By the prince of the demons, he casts out demons." The same miracle is read either as the arrival of Isaiah's promised age or as the work of Satan. This charge will come to a head in chapter 12, where it becomes the unforgivable sin (12:31–32). Matthew thus stages the great controversy of the Gospel: who is this man? Every reader is invited to choose an interpretation — and the choice is not neutral.

#### 6. Compassion and the Harvest (9:35–38)

The chapter closes with a summary that gathers its threads: "Jesus went about all the cities and the villages, teaching in their synagogues and preaching the Good News of the Kingdom, and healing every disease and every sickness." Here is his triple office — teacher, preacher, healer — an echo of Isaiah 61's servant who brings good news to the brokenhearted.

When he sees the multitudes, "he was moved with compassion" — the Greek *splanchnizomai* pictures a gut-wrenching pity, the same word used of God's heart for his people in Ezekiel 34. The crowds are "harassed and scattered, like sheep without a shepherd," language drawn from the prophets' indictment of Israel's failed leaders (Ezek. 34:5–6; Ps. 23). And so comes one of the most practical verses in the Gospels: "The harvest indeed is plentiful, but the laborers are few. Pray therefore that the Lord of the harvest will send out laborers into his harvest."

Notice the order: before sending, Jesus prays. The church's first missionary act is intercession. This word anticipates the sending of the seventy-two (Luke 10:1–2) and, ultimately, the Great Commission (28:19). Mission begins not with strategy but with a heart moved by compassion and lifted in prayer.

#### Thematic Threads and Application

Four threads bind the chapter together:

**Authority.** Every scene demonstrates that Jesus' word carries creative, forgiving, and life-giving power — over sin, disease, demons, and death itself. The question of his identity is never far from the surface.

**Mercy over ritual.** Table fellowship, fasting, purity boundaries — all are reoriented around the person of Christ. Holiness in the kingdom is not a wall kept up but a table opened wide.

**Faith as the point of contact.** In every healing, faith is where grace meets need; yet grace always comes first — Jesus *sees* their faith, and he calls Matthew before Matthew has done anything to deserve it.

**Compassion as mission.** The chapter ends where it began: with Jesus moving toward those who need him, and sending his disciples to do the same.

For the believer today, Matthew 9 is both mirror and mandate. It mirrors our own condition — we are the paralytic, the tax collector, the bleeding woman, the blind man; we needed a Physician, and he came. And it mandates our response: to carry others to Christ on beds of faith, to open our tables to those the world excludes, to pray without ceasing for laborers for the harvest, and to trust that in him forgiveness and healing, joy and mercy, are one.

### Chapter 10

#### Introduction: The Mission Discourse

Matthew 10 stands at a hinge in the Gospel. Chapter 9 has shown us Jesus' authority in action—over disease, over demons, over nature—and his willingness to call sinners (the centurion, the paralytic, the lepers, Matthew himself). Now he gathers the twelve and does what he has been doing all along: *sends*. The chapter is a mission discourse, and it functions on two levels. Historically, it records the commissioning of the apostles for their first circuit of Galilee. But because Matthew wrote to a church living under pressure from both synagogue and state, the words also speak directly to his readers: this is how God's people are sent, what they will face, and why they need not ultimately fear.

The governing theme is **the King sending his representatives into a hostile world**. Everything in the chapter—authority, instruction, warning, comfort—flows from that reality.

---

#### I. The Twelve Appointed (vv. 1–4)

> "He called to himself his twelve disciples, and gave them authority over unclean spirits..."

The number is not accidental. Twelve evokes the twelve tribes: in these men, Jesus is gathering a *new Israel*, a restored people around the true Son of David. The title "apostles" (from the Greek for "sent ones") will define their identity from this moment on. They are not merely followers; they are emissaries with delegated authority.

That authority—over unclean spirits, over every disease and sickness—is messianic in character. It is the power of the coming King, the Son of Man to whom the dominion of Daniel 7:14 belongs, now flowing outward through his servants. The miracles that will accompany their preaching are not ends in themselves but *signs* confirming that the message is true (cf. Heb. 2:3–4; Mark 16:20).

The roll call itself carries theology. Matthew the tax collector stands among them—grace has entered the inner circle. And yet so does Judas Iscariot, "who also betrayed him." Matthew's sober honesty reminds us that election and commission do not guarantee perseverance (cf. v. 22), and that the church from its first hour contains both grace and treachery.

#### II. The Commission and Its Conditions (vv. 5–15)

##### "The lost sheep of the house of Israel" (vv. 5–7)

The restriction to Israel is a *mission strategy*, not a permanent boundary. Jesus is not excluding the Gentiles; he is ordering the work. The gospel goes first to the people who have the promises, and only through their rejection will it break open to the nations (cf. Acts 1:8; Rom. 11; Matt. 28:19). "Lost sheep" is a deliberately tender image—Israel is not condemned here but *missed*, wandering, in need of the Good Shepherd.

The message is startlingly brief: **"The Kingdom of Heaven is at hand."** This is the same cry of John the Baptist and of Jesus' own ministry (4:17). The ingressive "at hand" means God's reign has *broken in*—it is not a distant hope but a present invasion, embodied in the person and work of Jesus. Everything else in the commission flows from this announcement.

##### "Freely you received, freely give" (v. 8)

Grace sets the economy of the mission. The gospel and its power cost the disciples nothing; therefore they are to charge no one. This single sentence is a charter against every later commercialization of the ministry.

##### Dependence on Providence (vv. 9–10)

No gold, silver, or bronze; no bag, no second coat, no sandals, no staff. The logic is twofold. First, the disciples are to live by *daily dependence* on God's providence and on the hospitality of those who receive them—the kingdom they announce is already present in the generosity of its hosts. Second, their empty hands are a sign: they bring nothing for sale, no baggage of self-sufficiency. "The laborer is worthy of his food" (cf. 1 Tim. 5:18) guards against exploitation while keeping the mission free.

##### Discernment and Peace (vv. 11–13)

They are to find out who in each town "is worthy" and stay there—a call to discernment, not social climbing. The greeting of peace is both blessing and test: *if* the household is worthy, let your peace rest on it; if not, let your peace return to you. The shalom they carry is real, but it does not force itself on unrepentant hearts.

##### Shaking off the dust (vv. 14–15)

Shaking the dust from one's sandals was a Jewish gesture of separation, declaring "I am not responsible for what happens here" (cf. Acts 13:51). The warning that follows is among the most severe in Scripture: it will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah than for the rejecting city. The reason is proportion—Gentile cities had less light; Israel's towns had the prophets, the temple, and now the King himself. Greater light means greater accountability (cf. Luke 12:47–48).

#### III. Persecution Announced, Comfort Given (vv. 16–25)

##### Sheep among wolves (vv. 16–22)

"Behold, I send you out as sheep among wolves." The image is devastating and true: the disciples are sent into a world that is predatory toward them, and they carry no weapons of their own. The counsel—**wise as serpents, harmless as doves**—is not a contradiction but a balance. *Apharmegoi*, the Greek word for "harmless," means "without guile." Be shrewd in your methods; be pure in your motives. Use all legitimate wisdom to avoid needless danger, but never adopt the wolf's nature.

The escalation of opposition is precise: delivery to councils (Jewish courts), scourging in synagogues, then governors and kings (Roman authorities). History would obey this word exactly—Stephen, Paul, and the church at large. But notice the purpose clause: **"for a testimony to them and to the nations."** Suffering is not the mission's failure; it is part of its witness.

When the hour comes, they are told not to be anxious about what to say, "for it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you." This does not promise eloquence or immunity from fear; it promises that the Father will not leave his witnesses speechless when it matters most (cf. 2 Tim. 4:17).

##### Family division and endurance (vv. 21–22)

"Brother will deliver up brother to death..." The echo of Micah 7:6 is unmistakable, but Jesus intensifies the ancient lament: allegiance to him will cut even the closest bonds. This is not a teaching that family love is evil; it is a warning that Christ's claim is *exclusive* and that the world—beginning at home—will resist it.

The promise that anchors it all: **"he who endures to the end will be saved."** Perseverance, sustained by grace, is the mark of genuine discipleship (cf. Rev. 2:10; James 1:12). And the practical counsel—flee to the next city—is not cowardice but prudence: the mission must continue, and no martyrdom is required where none is demanded. The urgency behind it ("you will not have gone through the cities of Israel until the Son of Man has come") speaks to Matthew's community, which was living in the shadow of judgment—whether that "coming" refers to the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70 or to the final parousia, the point stands: history is moving toward its climax, and the work must not stop.

##### The disciple and the teacher (vv. 23–25)

"A disciple is not above his teacher." To follow Jesus is to share his fate, including the charge that defined his ministry: *Beelzebul*. If they called the head of the household demon-possessed, the household should expect no less. The answer to such hatred is not fear but confidence in God's omniscience and final judgment: nothing covered will stay covered. What is whispered in the dark—both the truth now hidden by persecution and the words Jesus has entrusted to his people—will be proclaimed from the housetops.

The contrast of fears is decisive. Do not fear those who kill only the body; **fear him who can destroy both soul and body in Gehenna**. Gehenna—the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, long a place of burning refuse—stands here for final judgment. The point is not that God delights in destruction but that he alone holds ultimate authority over life and death, and to fear created powers more than the Creator is to mistake the map for the territory.

#### IV. Fear, Providence, and Confession (vv. 26–33)

The sparrow passage is one of the most tender in the Gospels. Two sparrows sell for an *assarion*—a small copper coin, a trifle; yet not one falls to the ground apart from the Father's will. If he counts the lives of the cheapest creatures and the very hairs of your head, then **"you are of more value than many sparrows."** This is not abstract providentialism; it is personal: the God who numbers your hairs is the God who will not lose you.

From that security flows the word about confession and denial. In a context of persecution, "confessing" Christ before men means open, costly profession—praying in his name, bearing his marks, refusing to disown him when it is cheaper to do so. The promise is reciprocal and grounded in the Father's own character: what he does with his witnesses flows from who he is (cf. Rom. 10:9–10; Rev. 3:5). Denial, by contrast, is a refusal that God will honor as seriously as confession.

#### V. The Cost of Following (vv. 34–39)

> "Don't think that I came to send peace on the earth. I didn't come to send peace, but a sword."

This verse is often quoted out of context, so it deserves care. Jesus is not describing himself as violent; he is describing the *effect* of his claim. The gospel divides households where loyalty to Christ collides with loyalty to family (cf. Luke 12:51). The "sword" is the edge of a truth that will not be compromised.

The three "worthy" sayings then state the priority plainly: father, mother, son, and daughter are good gifts of God, but none may take the place of Christ in the disciple's affections. To love them *more than* him is to fail at the one relationship that defines everything else.

**"He who doesn't take his cross and follow after me isn't worthy of me."** In the Roman world the cross was the symbol of shameful, criminal death. To "take up" one's cross was to accept a life of self-denial, exposure, and possible martyrdom in step with the crucified Lord (cf. 16:24; Luke 9:23). It is daily: the old self put to death so that Christ may live through us.

The paradox closes the section: **"He who seeks his life will lose it; and he who loses his life for my sake will find it."** The kingdom inverts the world's arithmetic. Clinging to self—reputation, comfort, safety—destroys it; surrendering self to Christ is the only way it is ever truly found (cf. John 12:25).

#### VI. Receiving the Sent Ones (vv. 40–42)

The chapter ends where it began—with sending and receiving. "He who receives you receives me, and he who receives me receives him who sent me." In one sentence Matthew compresses the unity of Father and Son in mission: to welcome a poor, unlettered apostle is to welcome Christ himself, and through him the Father (cf. John 13:20; 2 Cor. 5:20).

The rewards descend in order—prophet for prophet, righteous man for righteous man—until they reach the smallest act imaginable: **a cup of cold water** given to one of "these little ones" in the name of a disciple. The gospel's economy of grace runs all the way down. No kindness done for Christ's people is too small to be counted by him.

---

#### Conclusion: What This Chapter Means for the Church

Matthew 10 remains the church's charter for mission and its comfort under trial. Its lessons, in brief:

1. **We are sent with authority.** The gospel we carry is not our own; it is the King's reign breaking into the world, backed by his power.
2. **We are sent in dependence.** Empty hands, open hearts, trust in daily providence and in the generosity of God's people.
3. **We are sent into opposition.** The world will resist the message, beginning at home; suffering for Christ is testimony, not accident.
4. **We are sent without ultimate fear.** The God who numbers our hairs holds both soul and body in his hands; he will not leave his witnesses speechless or alone.
5. **We are sent at a cost.** First place for Christ, the cross taken up daily, self-surrender as the only road to true life.
6. **We are sent to one another.** The church is bound together by mutual reception and even the smallest acts of service done in his name.

Read across two thousand years, the chapter's portrait holds: a King who sends his sheep among wolves, who counts their hairs, who promises them his Spirit, his presence, and his coming—and who calls each one of us, as he called the twelve, to go.
